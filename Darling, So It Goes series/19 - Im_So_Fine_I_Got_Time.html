I'm So Fine (I Got Time)

Back when he was a child, young and innocent and so very naive, Buck used to like the Fourth of July. He remembered being entranced by the fireworks, by how bright and loud and colorful they were. It felt, kinda, like someone took everything that was bottled up inside him and allowed it outside to explode in a cacophony.

The appreciation for them was carried with him as he got older, never really going away as he traveled the hemisphere and settled down in Los Angeles. Not until life started throwing its curveballs at him. The first July after the ladder truck explosion was difficult, but it had nothing on the one after Eddie was shot, the way a firecracker being set off down the road had him tackling his then fiance to the ground before he even registered what he was doing.

Eddie had it worse, even though he tried not to show it at first. The suppressed flinching at every loud bang, the worsening nightmares. The day was an experience in suffering for him, however stoic he tried to be throughout it. To the point that, if it was getting to him bad enough, Bobby would keep him the man behind. 

Now, too, there was Hoover, who would whimper and whine and try to hide when the idiots of their city set off fireworks. And there was Gavin, who didn’t enjoy the noises any more than his fathers or the dog. The only one truly unperturbed by it was their cat, Senorita, because - although they themselves didn’t flinch every time a rocket whistled through the air or a bang echoed between houses - Christopher and Abuela still had to watch it slowly fray away at everyone else’s nerves.

It did not help any, either, that people could be idiots. And, when they were idiots, it usually meant more work for Buck and the others. Idiots playing with explosions meant that it was doubled, tripled, maybe even quadrupled. After shift after shift spent putting out fires started by errant sparks and responding to medical calls where people had blasted off their entire hand, Buck was reaching the end of his patience.

And unfortunately for everyone, he could do something about that now.

“Hold this,” he barked at Ravi after the engine was parked and everyone else was piling out, thrusting his phone at the younger man. “You’re playing cameraman.”

“Uh, okay.” Ravi fumbled the phone a bit, glancing about with the obvious hope that someone would come rescue him. Except Albert was gone, busy transporting a patient with Hen and Chimney, and Toby was scurrying quickly away. There was no one that was going to swoop in and save him from Interim Captain Diaz.

“Eddie, come here.” Buck grabbed his husband by the arm and dragged him to stand at his side. “Okay, Ravi, start recording.”

Ravi sighed, tapped at the screen, and then nodded.

“Hi!” Buck chirped, clapping his hands together. The smile he wore felt like it was splitting his face, and he knew he looked vaguely murderous from the way Ravi flinched. “Eddie and I would just like to remind all of you, once again, on behalf of the Los Angeles Fire Department, that fireworks are illegal in the city of Los Angeles. Even the “safe and sane” ones. All fireworks, all of them.

“I know they’re pretty, I know they’re loud and we all like it when things go boom. Except no, no we don't. We hate it when things go boom. It upsets our dog, it makes our kid cry. It makes us twitchy. We just got back from the sixth call out this week - it’s only Tuesday! - for someone losing a body part because they were playing with explosives. We are still firmly in red flag conditions, with multiple wildfires just outside the city, and it wouldn’t take much for there to be one in the city. So please, for all that is fu-”

“Mi sol.” Eddie placed a hand on his shoulder, and Buck heaved in a breath.

“So please,” he said, a bit calmer. “Stop it. Thank you.”

Ravi, eyebrows skyhigh, thumbed the phone screen again before handing it over to Buck.

“How long were you keeping that in, Cap?” he asked, keeping a healthy distance.

“Since one in the morning, when someone decided to set off a mortar five blocks away,” Buck answered, trimming the video and uploading it to their official Instagram account. “It woke up Gavin.”

It also woke up Eddie, who found himself catapulted from a dream into a waking nightmare, momentarily convinced he was back in Afghanistan. He’d rolled himself and Buck off the bed, shoving the pair of them under the safety of cover until Buck managed to get him back to himself. Suffice to say, none of them slept very well afterwards.

“I’m thinking about ordering a bunch of pizzas today, because I do not feel like cooking,” Buck said as he tucked his phone into his pocket and headed for the stairs, Eddie and Ravi falling into step behind him. “Where did Toby go? I’ll ask him what he wants.”

Since Toby kept kosher, he was usually the one that they took into consideration when determining what to do for dinner. The rest of the crew pretty much went at the food like a pack of starved beasts, eating anything and everything that was set down in front of them. He’d probably be okay with a cheese pizza all to himself, but Buck felt more comfortable asking the man instead of just assuming.

“Pretty sure he found someplace to hide,” Ravi said. “I’ll go find him.”

“Hide from what?” Buck asked, but the younger firefighter was already jogging off. “He’s not hiding from me, is he?”

“We’ve both been on edge today,” Eddie pointed out. “You nearly bit that woman’s head off earlier.”

“They were shooting Roman candles at each other!”

It was all fun and games, until the damn things malfunctioned and exploded in someone’s hand. Then it was nothing but blood and screaming and bits of burnt, mangled flesh dangling from an arm. Buck was so done with it all.

Upstairs, some of the others had migrated to the couches and were watching the news. Buck initially didn’t pay it much mind, instead going to add another tally mark to the “Firework Maiming” count that Mayfair, the C shift captain, started on a dry erase board. Except he heard Hen’s voice, and he knew immediately what the news was showing.

Taylor Kelly, once Buck pulled her in to help, had gotten to work quickly and with no small amount of malevolent glee. He was under no delusion that she was doing all of this out of the kindness of her heart, because she definitely wasn’t. The chance to earn some credit to her name by ruining a politician’s career was just too tempting for her to pass up, which was the exact reason he’d dangled that in front of her. And she had been a busy little bee ever since.

It started, firstly, with a quick and dirty and very merciless story about Councilwoman Ortiz’s son. Taylor’s story had included, but was not limited to, the fact that he’d been very high and very drunk on the night he wrecked his expensive (and it was expensive, Taylor made sure the viewers were aware of just how much it cost) sports car. She’d even managed to get an interview with one of the two women he’d crashed into and nearly killed. And then, after that, was the interview with Hen and Karen themselves. That was what the news was showing clips of once more.

“She’s an innocent little girl,” Karen was saying, talking about Mara as best she could without violating privacy laws. “She doesn’t deserve to be the victim of an angry politician trying to hurt people.”

“Y’know, I still hate Taylor,” Eddie remarked, standing beside Buck. On the television, the news had switched over to a clip of a reporter chasing the councilwoman down for comments.

“But?” Buck prompted, because it sounded like that statement had a but after it.

“No, no buts,” his husband said. “I still hate her. Full stop.”

Buck just rolled his eyes.

It was only a couple short years ago that simply being around Chimney made Eddie’s old fight club instincts flare up. Now, he sat with him and Maddie, happily chatting away. He and Buck had come over with the boys, ostensibly so the brother and sister could spend time together. In practice, Buck had been dragged away to have a tea party with Jee-Yun and Mara. Chris and Gavin trailed after them, the latter because he wanted to play, too, and the former because he undoubtedly saw an opportunity to torment his step-father.

“Mara seems to be settling in alright,” Eddie remarked, turning slightly in his seat at the sound of giggles from deeper in the house. “How has it been?”

After Ortiz wrecked the Wilsons’ lives, Maddie and Chimney wanted to help just as much as everyone else. Their solution was a bit more constructive than Buck’s rain of fire approach, and made possible by the fact that they had the space. They’d applied to be an emergency placement, getting Mara out of the group home she’d been stuck back into. Eddie just didn’t know how long this would last, given that it was an emergency placement. That sounded rather temporary to him, which was the point, but he hoped that it lasted long enough for the Wilsons to get their fostering license back.

Maddie shared a look with Chimney, and then sighed.

“Awkward at times,” she admitted. “We had Hen and Karen over the other day, and that went wonderfully. Until they left. And then Mara got quiet and went into her room.”

“How are things on your home front?” Chimney asked. “Buck talking to Bobby yet?”

Eddie knew that Buck would have been okay with Bobby retiring, knew that the specter of that had been hanging over them ever since they were waiting for their flight home from Saint Paul’s and Bobby first brought up the idea of Buck being captain in his stead. It wasn’t Bobby retiring that hurt Buck so much, although it did hurt. It was the fact that Bobby did it without any word to anyone first, it was the fact that all signs pointed to Bobby planning to leave, in some unspeakable but permanent way. It was the fact that for Buck, it probably felt like all too familiar abandonment.

Every Sunday Dinner since that revelation came with a wintry front. Bobby was invited, Bobby would always be invited and expected, especially after they spent so long in a hospital waiting room, hoping and praying that the man would wake up. Buck wouldn’t just cut him off, no matter how angry he was, not so soon after they almost lost him forever. But he could, and he did, give a cold shoulder that rivaled Christopher at his moodiest.

And every single time, Bobby would look like a kicked puppy whenever Buck gave him the blandest greetings possible.

“Not really,” Eddie finally answered. “Just what you’ve seen on Sundays. I’m pretty sure Chris has talked to him more. I don’t know how much longer Athena’s gonna be patient about it, before she’s shoving them into a room together.”

“Maybe we should get them into couple’s counseling,” Maddie suggested.

It was said as a clear joke, but honestly? Eddie was very tempted to go the route. It would just be a matter of managing to get both men to show up unawares. Easy to achieve with Buck, probably somewhat more difficult with Bobby.

“Oh, look,” Chimney suddenly said, reaching for the remote and turning up the volume on the TV. It had been playing in the background the entire time until now, muted and ignored. “It’s your favorite news reporter.”

Eddie made a face and flipped him off, which made the man laugh. On the screen, Taylor Kelly’s stupid face was staring back at them.

“...as we continue our expose on Councilwoman Ortiz’s flagrant misuse of power,” she was saying. “Though she is still hiding from the public she claims to serve, others have not been so reticent.”

The footage cut to Deidra, perched primly on a chair.

“I’ve known Karen and Henrietta Wilson for years,” Deidra told the camera. “Since they first applied to be foster parents. I cannot tell you how many children they’ve welcomed into their homes with open arms, children that had nowhere else to go. It takes very special people to do what they do. Or did, I suppose I have to say now.”

“Where would those children have ended up instead?” Taylor’s voice came from off screen. “When Ortiz ordered their license revoked, what happened to the little girl they were hoping to adopt?”

“I can’t precisely tell you that,” Deidra answered. “But she would have gone to a group home, at least at first, until another family was able to take her in. However long that took, which could very well be months. Maybe even longer.”

“What can you tell us about these group homes?”

“I can tell you that they’re overcrowded. I told you, the Wilsons took in children that would’ve had nowhere else to go except these group homes. And there are so many of them. The children, I mean. We don’t have the resources to make sure they’re properly taken care of in the way that they, as individuals, need. We don’t have the space. We depend on each and every foster parent to make the difference, on each and every person that decides to take these kids in with open arms and give them the family they deserve. I just wish…”

“You wish?” Taylor prompted, when Deidra trailed off with a sigh.

“I just wish the councilwoman would focus more on helping these children, instead of hurting them.”

“So I think we can all agree that she’s still a bitch,” Maddie said, pausing for Eddie’s vocal agreement. “But I’m kinda liking what she’s doing.”

“Don’t compliment her.” Eddie knew he was whining, just a little. “It gives me hives.”

“Oh, c’mon, Eddie.” Chimney reached over and thumped him on the arm. “She’s using her evil wiles for good this time.”

“Babe, are you complaining about Taylor again?”

Buck’s voice came from behind them. The three of them turned and then, as a single entity, did their best to pretend like they’d been stricken with a coughing fit rather than the laughter they were trying to cover up. Buck, with his face covered in enough makeup to almost qualify as a clown, just let out a sigh.

“Yeah, yeah, let it out.” He reached up to, presumably, rub at his eye, only to pause at the last moment. It was probably a good thing, because it would just smear the bright red eyeshadow. “You’re all just jealous.”

“You look very pretty,” Maddie said while Chimney gave in fully to the laughter, bending over where he sat and clutching at his stomach.

“The prettiest,” Eddie loyally said. “Make all the other clowns jealous.”

“He- He looks like-” Chimney gasped like a dying fish. “He looks like Ronald McDonald.”

“You all know just how to make a man feel comfortable with himself,” Buck drawled. “I can’t wash this off until after we leave, can I?”

“They’ll probably be upset if you do,” Eddie said. He pulled out his phone and, not even bothering to hide what he was doing, took a quick picture of the other man’s face.

“Are you posting that to Instagram?” his husband asked, his attempt at a glare losing its heat with the amount of blush he had on.

“No,” Eddie answered. Buck let out a relieved sigh. “I’m sending it to TK and Carlos.”

“...It’s whale watching season, you know.”

“Worth it.”

Ever since they first took in Gavin, Buck and Eddie made a concerted effort to do things with just Chris. Sometimes that fell through, but Buck thought they did a good job of keeping to it. Especially with the way that, as he’d gotten older, Chris decided he was too cool for most of what they’d used to do. Or at least he pretended that was the case. It turns out that, no matter what they claimed, a thirteen year old was just as excited to go to the zoo just as they were when they were twelve, and when they were eleven, and so on.

“Okay, so, we can either go that way and start with the reptile lair first, or that way and start with the aviary,” Buck said, without even glancing at a map. They’d been here often enough that they knew their way around the place. “Up to you, Chris.”

The kid just shrugged, the very image of unaffected. Buck and Eddie met gazes behind his back and rolled their eyes.

“Well I’d like to see the birds,” Eddie said, as haughtily as possible.

“There’re birds everywhere, Dad,” Chris told him, matching his tone.

“There might be someone with a snake out in the lair,” Buck suggested. “We can make your dad hold it.”

Chris turned slightly on his crutches to eye him speculatively, and then he nodded before taking the left arm of the Y they were standing at. At Buck’s side, Eddie let out a sigh and then reached out to pinch him.

“Ow!” Buck yelped and glared at him. “What was that for?”

“You know damn well what.”

“Oh, it’s just a snake, you big baby.” He poked his husband in the side. “You’re not Chimney, don’t pretend like you’re bothered by them.”

“Doesn’t mean I want to hold one.” Eddie’s eyes flickered up to his hair and he reached up, tugging at a loose curl. “Any reason for this today?”

“Not really.” Buck shrugged, resisting the urge to run his fingers through his hair. He hadn’t put any product in that morning, so the curls were free. “Just… I dunno. Wanted a change.”

“I like it,” Eddie told him. “It looks good.”

“You’d think I look good no matter what, your opinion is biased.”

His husband shrugged, not bothering to deny the accusation.

“Dad! Pops!” Chris was standing several feet away from them, looking very annoyed. “Hurry up!”

“Five minutes ago, he was acting like being here was a bother,” Eddie said under his breath as the pair made their way to their son. “Now look at him. He gets the bossiness from you.”

“Oh, screw you,” Buck shot back.

“I don’t think they want that kind of snake loose here, mi sol.”

The scornful glare from Chris, when Buck shoved a laughing Eddie into a tree, was well deserved. And yes, they did end up holding a snake.
