Where You Belong (Inside My Arms)

The Christmas party was a success. Not that Buck ever really had any doubt about it, but still. Here he was on Christmas Day, at home and surrounded by his family. It was everything he had ever wanted as a child, and it made him all warm and fuzzy inside as an adult. The only problem was, well…

There was Bobby and Athena, with Michael and the kids; there was Hen and Karen and Denny; there was Maddie and Chimney, taking that step forward; there was Eddie and Chris with his abuela.

Buck was surrounded by his family, but they all had their own families within it. Not that he held any of that against them, obviously. What kind of heartless monster would be upset over their loved ones being loved? Just old insecurities, rearing their heads once again. The past year really gave them fuel to last forever.

Listen, Buck never once regretted fighting for his job. Bobby was in the wrong, had pulled a dick move in keeping him from his job. There was no debating that, it was what it was. And that was after he spent months and months alone in his apartment, unable to spend as much time with his family as he wanted to. That was the core of it, really, the burning desire to be with his family again combined with the absolute terror of being tossed aside like he always seemed to be. 

(He could remember how he felt, when he saw Bosko’s name taped over his own. The agony, the rage, the terror. The way he found himself chanting and shouting and crying the same word in his mind, over and over: No. No, no, no, no.)

But the methods? If he could do it all over again, Buck would definitely change those. Maybe make a bigger effort to go through the department’s union. Try to go over Bobby’s head, since he deemed it appropriate to go behind his back. He definitely should have gotten a better lawyer. Although maybe Mackey deserved more credit, because the city had offered him a seven digit settlement. 

Mostly, though? Buck would have spoken to Eddie first. If he changed nothing else, he would have changed that. Because Eddie and Chris had both deserved better. Because now things between them were… Better, but not great? Broken, but healing?

Fragile. They were fragile. That’s a good word for it. Fragile and delicate, and Buck knew what could happen if he pushed too hard and asked for too much before things were settled. So he didn’t push and he didn’t ask for anything, even if sometimes he couldn’t stop himself from being stupid and flirting in kitchens. But his feelings were so big and clumsy, and he figured he could be excused for the way they escaped him at times.

A year ago, Buck would have happily walked over to join the Diazes without hesitation. He would have done it half a year ago, although it would have been more of a hobble than a walk. Today, instead, he turned and made his way down the loft’s stairs and to the lockers, intent on stashing away the fake sprig of mistletoe he had been using to torment (almost) everyone.

He was almost there, rounding the trucks with the loft out of sight, when Eddie’s voice had him freezing and turning in place.

“Running away from your party?”

“It’s not my party, I just helped plan it,” Buck said. “Shouldn’t you be with Chris and your abuela?”

“Saw you slinking off, decided to follow.”

“You make it sound like I’m off to do something nefarious.”

“One never knows, I’ve heard the stories.” Eddie was grinning, his canines showing, and Buck rolled his eyes at the audacity of the man in front of him.

“I was going to put this away in my bag, you jerk,” Buck said, waving the mistletoe around a bit. He watched, breath stuttering, as Eddie gently grabbed him by the wrist and took it from him. His skin burned where the other man was touching him, and he hoped to god he wasn’t blushing as bad as he knew he was.

“It took me a long time to understand why I was so angry,” Eddie said, apropos of nothing. “About the lawsuit, I mean.”

“Eddie?”

“Shannon asked for a divorce, that night we went out. Did I tell you that? She wanted to leave again, and I was so hurt and angry. And then she died, and I felt guilty for being angry, so I buried it until it was gone.” Eddie’s fingers spun the mistletoe around, but his gaze was stuck on Buck’s. “And then you filed that lawsuit and it was like all that anger had never gone away.”

“And I’m so sor-”

“I was never really angry with you,” Eddie interrupted, barreling through the latest of Buck’s apologies. “Or, I was, but only because it made me feel like I had that night. I was angry because the possibility of you leaving reminded me so much of Shannon leaving.”

“What are you saying, Eddie?” Buck asked, barely daring to breathe, because surely not. He had stopped believing in Christmas miracles long before his first festive postcard to Maddie had gone unanswered. But as he watched, Eddie slowly raised his hand over their heads, the mistletoe dangling from his fingers.

Kissing Eddie was like coming home. It was warmth and it was fireworks and it was the waves cresting upon the beach. At some point, he felt the mistletoe bouncing off his head as Eddie’s hands went to his hair, his shoulders, his waist. At some point, his back was pressed against the firetruck and his front was pressed against Eddie. At some point, the world ceased to exist beyond Eddie’s mouth on his and Eddie’s hands on him and his hands on Eddie.

The kiss seemed to last forever and yet not long enough. Eddie pulled away, Buck leaning forward to chase after him, both of their chests heaving for air.

“I don’t ever want you to leave,” Eddie whispered.

“Never,” Buck answered, resting his forehead against the other man’s. “For as long you’ll have me.” 

For as long as you’ll stay, he didn’t say.

“Come to mine, after shift?” Eddie asked, as if there was ever any other possibility. “We’ll talk, together and with Chris.”

“You want Chris to know?” The question caused Eddie to look at him as if he was an idiot before he stepped away. Buck whined at the loss.

“C’mon, we’ve chanced it as is, I want to spend time with our family while we have it.”

“Our family?”

“Yes, Buck.” Eddie’s roll of his eyes was nothing but fond as he took Buck’s hand in his and led him back to the loft. “Our family.”

“Are you sure they know?”

Eddie’s question comes at the end of their first shift after The Well, after they’d changed and headed out with their work bags. It had been a mostly calm - not quiet, never quiet - shift, and a short one at only twelve hours. And through all twelve hours, there was not a single comment made. No teasing, no quips. Although at one point early on in the day, after they’d come in together, Buck had seen Chimney open his mouth to say something only for Hen to elbow him into silence, so maybe they were being kind and considerate? Bobby didn’t even split them up on the calls they’d gone on.

Really, it seemed like all of Buck’s fears had been for naught.

“They have to know, Eddie,” he said. “I was literally trying to claw through the Earth to get to you. They’d have to be oblivious idiots not to know by now.”

That was a possibility. A very likely possibility, what with the younger man’s utter inability to stay out of his partner’s personal space. Sure, there’d been the agreement at the beginning on no PDA during work hours, part of the whole ‘be as professional as possible to keep Bobby on-side’ idea, but that had mostly equated to not making out at the station. Or on the station roof. Or in the firetruck. 

“It’s probably your fault, then,” Buck said, throwing his bag into the back of the jeep. “Your oral fixation and the blood thinners were a colorful combination.”

“You weren’t complaining at the time.”

“What sane person would?”

Still, if there was one good thing to come out of The Well, it was the freedom of no longer having to hide. They didn’t even need to make a big announcement or whatever, not if everyone already knew. They could carry on as they had been, but without second-guessing every action they took together in front of the others.

So, the world continued turning and life continued on. Whatever concept of personal space Buck and Eddie had at the station was tossed out the window entirely. Most days, it wasn’t unusual to have one of them lounging against the other on the couch between calls. Buck plated Edde’s food, Eddie brought him coffee from down the way, they carpooled nearly every morning. Buck even helped wrangle a bunch of children the first time Chris hosted a sleepover, and no one batted an eye at the announcement.

But again, as always, life wasn’t all about the good times.

One night, with Eddie working a double, Chris at his abuela’s, and everyone else busy with their own lives, Buck was left adrift and with an excess of free time. Still keyed up from the shift he had left, he found himself at one of the many hook-and-ladder bars in the city. And he found his worst fears, personified.

Red Delacroix, real name John, was a firefighter who had retired after forty years. Earned his nickname for being a bit of a hothead when he was younger. The man had made the job his life, his coworkers his family, and now? Now he was drinking alone at a bar, sharing stories with an utter stranger because he had no one else to talk to.

“We’re all gonna go eventually, kid,” the man had told him. “You just don’t want to be the last one left.”

It was a sad statement, the entire conversation that night was sad. They talked about his old firehouse - the 134 - and he shared a photo of all of them, pointing out faces and adding names. The faces of the ones he’d lost, either to the dangers of the job or the march of time. He talked about Cindy, the one who’d got away, and Buck ached for him.

Naturally, when he tried to bring it up with the others during their next shift, the reaction was lukewarm, at best.

“So it’s all about you?” Chimney accused, which while probably fair, was far more hurtful than he had (hopefully) intended.

“Life gets busy,” Hen reasoned, which while definitely true, did jack to assuage Buck’s fears. 

He had Eddie now, but once he’d had Abby, and so maybe he was waiting for the other shoe to drop and for his partner to decide that going back to a life without him was what he really wanted. So Buck maybe, possibly, tried too hard to fix Red’s situation in a futile, projecting attempt to prove to himself that he wouldn’t end up the same way.  Inevitably, it all went wrong.

Cindy had dementia, and Red was still alone, and everything about it made Buck feel awful.

“Abby’s mom would have moments like that,” he told Maddie later.

“You still think about her a lot?” Maddie asked.

“Sometimes,” he admitted. “But that isn’t what this is about.”

“You don’t think this is hitting a little close to home?” she prodded. “A lonely hero firefighter who’s pining after his lost love?”

“You think I’m lonely?” he asked, confused. “I have Eddie, and Christopher.”

And Maddie gave him a look. A weird look, almost pitying, almost sympathetic, except not quite. Why would she look at him like that? Did she know something he didn’t? But instead of explaining, she said,

“I know how hard this is, seeing Red so alone.”

“Do you?” Buck asked. “No offense, but you’re never the one getting left behind. You’re always the one doing the leaving.”

Maddie didn’t linger much longer after that.

The week continued on. Hen did something risky and lifesaving, and which would have definitely gotten her fired if she’d been him. Red died, but not before Buck managed to do one good thing and ensure he didn’t die alone and unnoticed. Maddie came back, and promised he wouldn’t be the one left behind. It was a pinky promise, and those were sacred, and so he let himself believe her.

“That won’t happen to us, mi sol,” Eddie announced later, the two of them curled up on the couch, Chris in bed. “No matter what happens, you won’t be alone.”

“You say that now-”

“No, Evan, listen to me,” and the use of his name, his actual name, had his attention on a hook. “That will not happen. In the well, I was thinking about what would happen to Chris-”

“Eds-”

“And to you, if I didn’t make it out. So, last week, I went to my attorney. I changed my will.” Hands framing Buck’s face, he looked him straight in the eyes, the blue going watery with unshed tears. “If something happens to me, if I die, Christopher goes to you.”

“H-how does that even work? D-don’t you need my consent?”

“They said you could refuse, but-”

“Never,” Buck snarled, the two-syllable word a promise on his lips. “But what about your parents? If it came to that, wouldn’t they fight for him?”

“Probably, but I know nobody would ever fight for my son as hard as you.” He laid a gentle kiss on Buck’s birthmark, a newfound habit of his. “You’re family, Evan. You’ll always be family, and you’ll always have us.”

And Buck was an ugly crier, but at least only Eddie was around to see it then.

Chapter Notes

They all adjusted to the newest new normal in the weeks following the prison riot. Eddie and Buck started attending therapy sessions together, following the latter’s breakdown at the hospital. Before, they had been attending sessions over video on their own, but Copeland thought it would be helpful to talk with them as a couple. 

“You both have gone through a lot of shared trauma,” she had told them, at their very first in-person appointment together. “And so your recovery should be shared, as well.”

Chris was still going to therapy, having sessions with a Dr. Beaumont every other week. And it was very much needed, because it quickly became evident that his son had developed some very severe anxiety regarding Eddie and Buck’s mortality. He shuddered to think what he would have done if he didn’t have Copeland and Beaumont both talking them through it. Panic, probably, and then make an awful, impulsive decision, like quitting his (admittedly dangerous) job without any thought towards the short and long term consequences it would have on his finances.

Gracias a Dios, that did not happen.

At work, he was back by Buck’s side, full time. Albert had gotten his paramedic certification, and Bobby had been hard at work trying to steal him away from the station he’d been at. The younger Han had come over willingly, because at least with them, he was actually being taken out on calls. Hen complained, a little, but she was too overjoyed at not being stuck with Eddie any longer to truly be annoyed with training Baby Han.

“What did you do to her?” Buck asked, just after Albert got transferred to them. “She was annoying Bobby about getting a new paramedic almost as much as I was.”

“I did what Copeland and you both have been telling me to do for months,” he answered. “I opened up to others.”

“And it was that bad for her?”

“I might have overshared. A little.” Eddie shrugged. “But it got results.”

Ravi was still on A-shift and to the younger man’s relief, a Buck with Eddie by his side was a much happier, more tolerable teacher. Edde had even started helping his fiance with training him, and then they started including Albert whenever Hen was done with him. This led to the two probies forming a quick, strong, and distressingly familiar friendship.

“Is this how we were?” Buck asked him after the two watched Ravi and Albert repeatedly sneak glances whenever they thought the other wasn’t looking. “Were we this bad?”

“I refuse to believe that,” Eddie said. “But we were probably worse.”

“I feel like I need to give a shovel talk to one of them, but I don’t know which.”

“Or, we could do nothing and just leave them be while we bother Bobby and Hen upstairs.”

“That is also an option,” Buck agreed. “Hey, who gave you the shovel talk? Was it Maddie?”

“No, it was Athena, actually.”

“Oh, wow, I am so sorry, babe. I got one from Pepa and Abuela both. At the same time.” Buck shuddered. “I hadn’t realized ladies their age could be so scary.”

“Ladies their age? Buck, what would Abuela say if I told her?”

He was constantly on the phone, and Eddie was certain he once walked in on a quickly cut off conversation with his mother. Not Margaret, Buck hadn’t spoken to either of his blood-related parents since the front half of the year, but Eddie’s mother. There was plotting happening, and Eddie wasn’t in on it, and it had him paranoid.

Not Albert and Ravi, they were just as confused when he asked, but Hen was constantly smirking at him and his Abuela was far happier than she usually was around the holidays and Christopher was just… Giggly.
