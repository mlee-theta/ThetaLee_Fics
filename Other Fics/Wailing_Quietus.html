Wailing Quietus

Once, during a late night research binge, Buck had read about the legend of the Banshee. A fairy woman who would herald a death in the family, either at home or afar, by wailing or keening. Or she would forewarn of death, a shrieking attempt to turn the doomed away from mortal peril. Some of the stories he had read said she was a virgin girl who had died a brutal death, and others that she was an evil witch who actively caused the deaths she foretold. It was said her voice could shatter glass.


It was one of the nuggets of information he read and shared and didn’t dwell on afterwards. Useless trivia in a cluttered brain. Unimportant.


And then he started screaming Eddie’s name.


The first time it happened, lightning had struck and buried the man under thirty feet of rain soaked earth. Buck’s first instinct, when he realized what had happened, had been to claw at the mud with his bare hands. He hadn’t realized he’d been screaming Eddie’s name until Bobby had pulled him away, until he’d stopped screaming and the silence was loud in his ears.


Of course, Eddie was fine in the end. He emerged from the nearby reservoir like a hero of Greek myth, returning victorious from the Underworld. Eddie was fine, and they did not talk about how he nearly wasn’t. They didn’t talk about how there was mud under Buck’s fingernails and how Buck’s throat was still sore from screaming and how for an unspeakable moment there Buck actually had thought he was dead. Eddie was fine, and they did not talk about it, and that was fine too.

Once, during a late night research binge, Buck had come across a list of uncommon words. Words like “convivial,” someone that is great company, and “interlocutor,” the participants of a conversation. Words like “pernicious,” habits and actions that were particularly dangerous, and “zeugma,” a figure of speech where one word had a double meaning within a sentence. Words like “quietus.” Derived from Latin, it was defined as a stillness or a pause; something that quiets or represses; a removal from activity. It was the final settlement of a debt.


It was a poetic term for death.


It had struck him as odd when he first read it, because for Buck, death had never been quiet. Death was marked by the shouting of orders and the crackling of flame. It was defined, in his mind, with shouts of pain and gasps for air, with creaking timbers about to give and begging words that fell on deaf ears. Death was marked with the drone of a flatline and the shrill notes of a PASS device and the lamentations of loved ones. Death, like life, had always been loud for Buck.


And then Eddie was shot.


The thing is, Buck could not tell you what it had sounded like. He knew, distantly, that there had been a gunshot. He knew it in the same way that he knew it snowed in Antarctica and that the surface of the sun would burn a person to a crisp. An academic knowledge, separate from himself. But Buck couldn’t tell you if it had echoed off the buildings around them, if it had been a pop or a crack, if the sound had lingered long after the bullet had passed.


Buck could write soliloquies, if he had the talent, on Eddie’s face and how it had slackened in surprise as the bullet pierced his back. He could, from memory, describe the way Eddie’s shoulder burst into a fountain of red that had arced across a distance that was not yet uncrossable. He could, with much hesitance, tell of the warmth that splattered across his skin like water and the way it had tasted of pennies in his mouth.


Buck could not, even in his dreams, tell you what any of it had sounded like.


Of course, Eddie was fine in the end. He woke in his hospital bed, much like Briar Rose in her bramble covered castle, whole and alive and beautiful. Eddie was fine, and they did not talk about how he nearly wasn’t. They didn’t talk about how Buck had spent days in a muffled world, with the taste of pennies in his mouth and hands that still felt tacky no matter how thoroughly he’d wash them. Eddie was fine, and they did not talk about it, and that was fine too.

Once, during a late night research binge, Buck had read about the legend of the Banshee. A fairy woman who would wail at death, who would warn of it wth her screams and tell of it with her keening. It was a nugget of information to be shared with his friends and then forgotten. Useless and unimportant, taking up space in an already cluttered brain.


And then he started screaming Eddie’s name.


The second time it happened had been after a day of being held hostage by a pair of convicted criminals on an escape attempt. One of them was arrested again, cuffed and hauled away moments after they crossed through the hospital entrance. The second was still outside in the ambulance with Eddie, and Buck was the most scared he’d been all day.


Buck had been anxious with the guns pointed at himself and Eddie, had been utterly pissed off at the mention of Christopher, but he hadn’t been scared through any of it. He had been with Eddie, and Eddie had been with him, and together they would get through anything and be home afterwards. Except they weren’t together now, Buck wasn’t with Eddie, and Buck was the most scared he’d been all day.


The moment Buck heard the gunshot, Eddie’s name had been torn from his throat. He kept crying it as he ran, his gut twisted with the fearful thought that this time would be it. This time would be the moment their luck broke. This time was their third strike, and neither were safe.


Of course, Eddie was fine in the end. He knelt there, snapping at officers to get away while he kept a dead man’s heart beating with his own hands, as he kept a promise to a murderer to settle a debt that wasn’t his. Eddie was fine, and they didn’t talk about how the sound of the gun echoed in Buck’s ears for hours after, how Buck had heard it and knew that Eddie was dead and kept knowing it until he saw him living. Eddie was fine, and they did not talk about it, and that was fine too.

Once, during a late night research binge, Buck had come across the word “quietus.” A stillness, something that quiets or represses; death. It had struck him as odd when he’d first read it, because death had never been quiet in Buck’s experience. It had struck him as odd, because for him, death was always accompanied by activity and noise.

And then Eddie had been shot.

He hadn’t died. He hadn’t died, and he’d been fine then and he was fine now. Eddie hadn’t died, but Buck thinks he might have, because the world had been so quiet ever since. The world had been muffled and made still. And sometimes the silence was so loud. Sometimes, the silence was filled with the echoing sound of gunfire that Buck didn’t remember and the sound of Eddie’s name leaving his lips in a cut off wail.

Sometimes the silence wasn’t filled with anything, and that was when the silence was at its loudest.

Of course, Eddie was fine. He had been fine after being buried alive, and he had been fine after being shot, and he had been fine after being held hostage. Eddie had been fine, and they didn’t talk about how Buck had wailed and dug and drug and pressed and pleaded and ran. Eddie had been fine, and they did not talk about it, and that was fine too.

They didn’t talk about how Buck spent every moment of every day with Eddie’s name stuck in his throat, waiting to be ripped out with a gunshot. They didn’t talk about how Buck’s mouth always tasted of pennies and how, after certain dreams, he’d stand in his bathroom scrubbing away at blood that had been washed from his hands months ago. They didn’t talk about how loud the silence could be.

They didn’t talk about how Buck felt more banshee than man, more dead than alive.

They didn’t talk about how, even though Eddie was fine in the end, Buck wasn’t.

Eddie was fine, and they didn’t talk about it.

And that was fine, too.
