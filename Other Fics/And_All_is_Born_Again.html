Chapter 1

His name was Eddie Diaz, and he loved a man named Buck. Always, in all ways. Forever and ever, amen.

His name was Eddie Diaz, and he loved a man named Buck. Those two things never, ever changed. Even when everything else did. Even when everything else was so warped as to be nearly unrecognizable.

Each and every single time, ever since he closed his eyes for the first last time and opened them again an impossible moment later, it happened the same way. He would close his eyes and open them to see Buck, like he had just blinked. His eyes would meet those beautiful blues, and he would remember… Everything.

The first time, it had been such a surprise. He’d freaked out, unable to understand what was going on. One moment he’d been letting out his last breath, and the next he was being introduced to a man he knew as well as he knew himself.

A man who did not know him.

The second time had been just as rough, but he’d figured it out by the third. Now, he rode the deluge of memories like a lazy river. He was there, and Buck was there, and everything else was simply a matter of going with the flow.

Eddie closed his eyes.

He opened them.

“I’d like to introduce you to everyone,” Bobby was saying. He was in uniform, a firefighter this time. He wasn’t always, not every time. Sometimes he was an actual chef, on the odd occasion a teacher or professor. But they were firefighters this time, just like the first time.

Eddie absentmindedly shook Hen’s hand and then Chimney’s as he sorted through what had been and what currently was. And this was apparently one of those lives: helicopter crash in Afghanistan; Shannon left him with nothing but a note; moved to Los Angeles to get away from his parents. It happened sometimes, a life so similar to his first one that he couldn’t figure out what, if anything, had been different before he opened his eyes. Like a Spot the Difference game on the hardest difficulty possible.

They were his favorite, honestly, if only because they were the easiest to adjust to.

(Was he taking over another Eddie Diaz’s life? He had wondered and wondered, before deciding it didn’t matter. He remembered their lives, every memory up to the point of opening his eyes, and so that made them, him. Or him, them.)

“And over there is Buck,” Bobby said, and when Eddie let himself look at him again, let himself look at him for longer and for more than that brief glance when he first opened his eyes, he saw a face he could draw from memory.

Buck was young, so very young, though not the youngest he ever knew him. There was that one life, where they crossed paths in elementary school of all places, and that was an era of awkwardness that Eddie would prefer not to dwell on. But the younger man was standing off on his own, brow furrowed and frowning. And when Eddie caught his eye and let his face be overtaken by a soft smile, Buck started glaring at him.

Not for the first time, he wished he’d woken up earlier, so he could stop Bobby from hyping him up so much.

It was a bit heartbreaking, that Buck at the beginning was just expecting to be dropped to the wayside, now that there was a (in his mind) better and fancier version available. That he had grown accustomed to it. The solid belief that he was nothing but spare parts, expendable and replaceable and only valuable for what he could give to others.

The ride to Eddie’s first official call-out was awkward. Not for him, not anymore. It had been the very first time, back when he was still the breaking army veteran and single father, doing his best to hold things together. Back when he was the Eddie Diaz trying desperately to play it cool and not make a fool of himself in front of his new coworkers. Now the awkwardness came only from the others trying to figure out how to act around the new addition, and Buck - stiff and unwelcoming - trying to kill him with his eyes.

(It was adorable.)

“So, Silver Star, huh?” Chimney leaned over Buck to ask. Everyone always wanted to ask about it, the moment they learned of it, and Eddie always hated it. Not as much as he used to, when the literal wounds were still fresh and inflamed, but it still took all he had not to snap at the other man. It reminded him far too much of his parents, who were too busy being proud of their son’s supposed heroism to see their son breaking before their eyes.

Eddie made a mental note to reach out to Miller and the others, the first chance he had.

“Yup,” he confirmed with a nod.

“You save a platoon or something?”

“Just a convoy.” A flippant answer that wasn’t anywhere near the traumatic truth, and rolled his eyes when the paramedic nudged at Buck with his elbow.

“Eddie.” Hen’s voice has him turning to her, and she’s leaning over the console. “You heard about the hot firefighter calendar?”

He doesn’t catch the look Buck must give her at the question, but the grin she shoots his way is nothing but shit-eatting. He remembered how into the calendar Buck had been, and currently was, and he knew the question was more about teasing him than Eddie’s actual interest in it.

“It’s for charity, right?” he asks, and she nods. And just because he loves Buck, and loves teasing Buck just as much, he says, “I was thinking of trying for it.”

“So is your full name Eduardo?” Buck interrupts to ask, just on the border of being confrontational.

“No,” Eddie says, trying very hard not to let it show how adorable he found the whole thing to be.

“Anyone ever call you Diaz?”

“Not my friends.”

“Something’s got to give,” Buck insists. “We got Cap, Hen, Chimney, Buck. We can’t just call you Eddie.”

“Why not?” he asks, and the younger man looked almost insulted at the question. “It’s a nickname, too.”

For the briefest of moments, he looks thrown, as if he hadn’t thought of that. Which, considering Maddie, perhaps he honestly did think ‘Eddie’ was what was on his birth certificate after being told it wasn’t short for ‘Eduardo.’ The moment passes and Buck is back to scowling, fueled by the way the others were laughing at him now. 

They get to the auto shop, with the guy impaled on the air nozzle and literally puffed up like a balloon. Together, they work to get him off and onto the ground, with Bobby giving orders that go nowhere, because they simply can’t get liquids or oxygen into the poor bastard. 

“Buck, I need you to get a fourteen-gauge angiocath,” Bobby instructs, and Eddie briefly wonders why he chooses Buck for it. It’s not like Hen and Chimney are unavailable, they’re just monitoring vitals. It’s not like Bobby is unaware of Eddie’s work history. He knows the captain trusts Buck - though not nearly as much as Eddie trusts Buck - but there’s just no reason for having Buck be the one to insert the angiocath. “We need to start decompressing the pleural cavity.”

Chimney actually has to move, has to stand and step away from the patient, to give Buck room to rummage through the medical bag. Which just emphasizes even more that Chimney could be the one doing this, instead of Buck being put on the spot. Seriously, what is the reason? Was this Bobby trying to let Buck show off? An attempt by the captain to reassure Buck of his place in the group?

Eddie loved Buck, but a medical call was not a situation where he shone the brightest.

“Want help?” he offers after Buck digs out the angiocath, already moving to unbutton the patient’s shirt.

“I got it.”

“I know.” And maybe his utter faith in the man leaked through his tone, because Buck pauses to glance at him, before getting the needle in place. “Lower would be better.”

“What? No. Second intercostal space, midclavicular line.”

“That’s the standard,” Eddie agreed, nodding, before gently moving Buck’s hand holding the angiocath to the fifth intercostal. “But the chest wall is thinner here, less chance of harming the patient any more.”

There’s a moment, where Buck is working his jaw and glaring at him, where he’s certain the man will tell him just where to stick the angiocath. It’s a delicate balance at this point, Eddie has learned, a tightrope walk to not push an already defensive Buck even further. But he knows the man, knows how to talk to him, knows what points to press.

And it takes every bit of strength in him not to smile when Buck ducks his head and says,

“Help me with his shirt.”

Soon, Eddie had the patient’s shirt opened and Buck had the catheter inserted, and they could all hear the sound of trapped air quickly escaping. The man gasped awake, much to everyone’s relief. And clean up afterwards is quick as the paramedics get him on the gurney and into the ambulance, so they were soon slamming closed the compartment doors of the truck.

“That was a good call, Eddie,” Bobby told him, a friendly slap on the shoulder as he went by, a statement accompanied by Chimney’s own,

“Nice job.”

“Yeah. Good call.” It’s given grudgingly, and Buck barely looked at him when he said it before climbing into the truck cab, but Eddie was going to take it anyway.

“How old are you?” Buck asked once, lifetimes ago. It was one of the few where Eddie had told him about the cycle he was in. Not because he ever thought Buck wouldn’t believe him - he always did, even if it took a while - but because the other man always started to feel guilty, somehow. They were curled up in bed when he asked, draped across each other like they were trying to fuse into one being.

“I don’t know,” Eddie admitted. He had lost count long, long ago. “Thirty-six, according to my driver’s license.”

“Funny.” Buck smacked him, a lazy movement of his hand against his chest that was barely anything. And then he asked, “Why don’t I ever remember?”

Buck always started to feel guilty, somehow.

“I don’t know,” Eddie said again. “I don’t even know why I remember.”

And then he kissed away whatever other questions were forming upon Buck’s lips, because there were no better answers to be given.

“Did you have a good first day?” Chris asked, as he got settled into bed.

After his first shift, a brief twelve-hour one, Eddie drove his poor, tired truck to Abuela’s. Once there, he spent a good ten minutes just hugging the both of them. He had seen them that morning, technically, but also…

But also.

“I did,” Eddie said, grabbing one of the books off his son’s shelf and squeezing himself onto the bed beside him. “I met a lot of really good people, and I helped some, too.”

“Good,” Chris said, smiling happily up at him.

Eddie wasn’t always a father, not in every lifetime. Sometimes, he’d meet Buck long before Chris was born. He didn’t actually care much for those lives, because there was always an impossible choice there. Well, a choice that wasn’t much of a choice.

Eddie’s love for Shannon was a guttered candle by this point, but he still cared too much for her to get in a relationship simply to have a kid. He’d thought of it once, that first time he opened his eyes and realized there was no Chris yet. But then he realized it was simply too close to that most unforgivable of acts, and hated himself for a long time after.

Thinking about Shannon reminded him that he needed to get in touch with a divorce lawyer, the sooner the better.

“Did you make any new friends?” Chris asked, tearing him out of his thoughts.

“I did,” Eddie told him, thinking of Buck. “We’re going to be the best of friends.”

Eddie did not often outlast Buck. It seemed that he was bound by fate to leave the other man behind, like so many others had done before him. Not due to a lack of effort on his part, because he would always do his best to cling to his mortality, even long after he accepted the cycle he was stuck in. But by accident or time’s inexorable march, he was almost always the first one who closed his eyes for the last time.

He felt guilty for it, but also relieved. Especially after the first time he was the one left behind. 

That lifetime had been the cruelest.

It had been cruel, because the first time he met Buck’s eyes, when Eddie remembered everything, had been at the scene of a car accident. Neither of them had been firefighters in that lifetime, with him a college student and Buck… He couldn’t remember what Buck had been in that one, assuming he had ever learned.

But there had been a car accident, and Eddie had run towards it to help. There he found Buck, pinned between tree and truck, and he had met wide and frightened blues. And with years of memories and experience flowing into him, he knew that there was no help to give, merely comfort.

That lifetime had been the cruelest, and also the shortest; there hadn’t been a Christopher to hold him back.

The last thing Eddie sees, when he closes his eyes, is Buck’s bright blues.

They’re the first thing he sees when he opens them again.

“Can I borrow a pencil?” Buck was asking him, leaning over from the previously unclaimed seat at his right. Eddie couldn’t help staring at how young he looked. “I know I had one, but I think I forgot it in my last class.”

“Y- Yeah, sure.” Eddie dug through his school bag, barely weirded out by the way his hands were smaller than they had been before he blinked. Smaller and mostly scarless and not as stiff. When he found his extra pencil, he handed it over. “Here ya go. You can just keep it.”

“Thanks, man.”

“So.” That was Shannon in the seat behind him. Eddie’s heart ached at the sound of her voice, because she was alive. Alive and young and very much not pregnant. He took a moment to mourn what - who - would never be. “You’re new. How far did you move?”

“Evan.” That answered that question, then. “And I’m sure he’s not that bad. I mean, he can’t be as bad as me.”

But he also remembered holding her hand as she died in the ambulance, and he remembered her throwing her glass at him when he asked for a divorce, and he remembered the lifetimes - a grand total of two - that he never met her at all.

“He is pretty,” Buck - Evan - agreed, and Eddie ducked his head.

He hated when they met so young. Not just because it meant Chris was not and would not be born, though that was the biggest, but also because of reasons like this. It didn’t matter that he was a teenager at the moment, he remembered being so much older, remembered being an old man. It was fine when they met as adults, but in cases like now? When Buck, when Evan didn’t even turn eighteen until after they graduated?

He was thankfully spared the effort of fumbling through a response, one that didn’t make him feel dirtier but also didn’t hurt B- Evan’s feelings, by the teacher getting up and starting class. It was the typical start of the year drivel, the same thing they were doing in every class that day, and Eddie sat back and barely skimmed over the syllabus that was handed out as Mr. Barnett went through it with them. He didn’t zone back in until the bell rang, setting them free for their next period.

Evan, predictably, left his borrowed pencil behind on his desk.

Eddie, just as predictably, chased after him to give it back.

If Eddie could just make it past graduation, make it past an arbitrary birthday or three, then maybe he could let himself start to flirt back and not feel so much like a dirty old man.

“No,” Eddie had blurted out, and then felt so awful as he watched those beautiful blues shutter in front of him. “I can’t.”

“Your parents’ names are Margaret and Phillip.” Because neither sibling actually talked about their parents, didn’t even have a picture of them up. But Evan just rolled his eyes, so Eddie continued on. “You hate the way your mother always says your name, exasperated and tired, and you hate the way that neither of them ever see you. How they look through you like glass.”

The anger burning in those blues faded away as they widened, but Eddie needed him to believe him, needed him to understand. 

“The first time you rode a bike, it was one Maddie found in the garage,” he said. “You fell, and your parents actually cared, so you started getting yourself hurt more. Maddie hated it, but you still did it because it had your parents actually noticing you, actually caring about you. Until it didn’t anymore.”

Eddie nodded.

“And all of that, that bullshit about dying and waking up again. That was- That was the truth.”

Another nod.

“You love me, don’t you?”

“I do,” Eddie said.

“Go to prom with me,” Evan said, and Eddie nearly screamed.

“Evan, I told you, I-”

“You’re secretly ancient, yeah, I’m getting that.” The teen rolled his eyes. “I’m not asking for sex, Eds, or even for you to kiss me. I just want you to go to prom with me.”

“Fine!” Eddie snapped, throwing his hands up. “Fine, we’re going to prom together.”

“Don’t sound too excited,” Evan grumbled, before stomping over to jam a finger into his chest. “There’ll be dancing, get that? There’ll be dancing and- And actually, there will be kissing! Because I’m not waiting until I’m a hundred for that.”

“Evan…”

“No! I don’t care that you really are an old man in a teenager’s body! You’re going to prom with me, and you’re going to kiss me, and you’re going to like it!”

Evan didn’t even give him the chance to argue further. He spun on his heel and stomped out of the room, leaving Eddie to slam his head into the wall in frustration.

This meant he had to go shopping for a tux, didn’t it?

It was, perhaps unsurprisingly, a difficult task to find time alone together after their graduation. After the last student had their name called, Eddie just managed to hunt down Evan before they were swept off by their respective families for a celebratory dinner and the party that followed. They were together, but they barely had time to even say hello because everyone else was constantly demanding their attention.

If it wasn’t Shannon, then it was one of their sisters, or it was Eddie’s parents. He had half a mind of accusing them of purposefully doing it, because neither his mother nor father were quite so warm towards Evan after he turned out to be Eddie’s mysterious prom date. But finally, and he figured he had Abuela to thank with the way she suddenly swooped at the perfect time to distract people, the two of them managed to flee the crowd for the warm night outside.

They were silent, simply enjoying the time to be together without someone else getting in the way, before Evan spoke up.

“Maddie’s been saving up to get a new car,” he said. “She’s gonna give me the jeep for my birthday.”

“Oh good, that means I can stop driving you everywhere.”

“Yeah, yeah, I can start chauffeuring you around, now. But uh, you know how I don’t really… Y’know, know what I want to do?” Eddie nodded, and Evan continued. “And- And I know we both applied for college, and that we both got in, but- But you’re secretly an old man, and you haven’t really said what you wanted to do, either.”

Eddie hadn’t put much thought to it, to be honest. Not the army, that’s all he really knew.

“I figured I’d take it easy,” he said, instead of voicing that.

“Exactly!” Evan said, pointing at him. “So I was thinking… Gap year? Take a road trip together, you and me?”

“A road trip where?”

“I… I hadn’t really thought that far ahead,” Evan mumbled, kicking at the dirt with his shoe. 

Eddie laughed.

“Y’know,” he said, “I’ve actually never been to Peru…”

The first time that Eddie opened his eyes after what should have been his final breath, he freaked out. He was struck by a full-blown panic attack, unable to understand how he went from an old man on his deathbed to a young man in a grocery aisle. Unable to understand why Buck didn’t recognize him, why the love of his life looked at him as if he was a complete and utter stranger. A mentally unwell stranger, at that.

It was an impression that he never quite managed to shake in that life, especially because he himself began to wonder if he was insane. Buck humored him, when Eddie managed to give him a shaky and disjointed explanation. Considering how much of the other man’s personal life Eddie could rattle off, even if it wasn’t entirely correct that time around, he should have been written off as a psychotic stalker. But luckily for him, Evan Buckley was very rarely reasonable in regards to his own personal safety.

The pair of them peacocking around at the station for an entire week, riling one another up until they exploded an ambulance, was a much better start to their friendship.

The second time it happened, Eddie still panicked, but it wasn’t from confusion. No, the panic was from the soul-crushing despair that came with the realization that it wasn’t just a one-off. He died and opened his eyes again and knew that this wasn’t going to be the last time it happened. He knew that it was going to happen again and again and again. He could see it all before him, an unending cycle of meeting Buck, being with him and growing old together, only to begin again at the start after he breathed his last.

Both a blessing and the worst possible curse he could think of.

Brown eyes met bright blues.

Standing on the Santa Monica pier, Eddie remembered everything.

“Chris,” he said, getting his kid’s attention. The boy was sitting on the bench where he’d left him before getting them both cotton candy. There hadn’t been anyone else there before. “You’ve made a friend?”

“I did!” was Chris’ cheerful reply as he took the snack from his father’s hand. Buck was looking increasingly panicked and flustered, clearly expecting to be yelled at, clearly expecting Eddie to run him off. “His name’s Buck! He’s a firefighter, too!”

“Is he now?” He held out a hand, the one not holding his cotton candy. “Eddie Diaz.”

“Uh, Buck. Buckley,” the love of his life introduced himself, shaking his hand, and then flushed. “Evan Buckley. Sorry, I didn’t mean to overstep.” Like that was possible for any version of him at this point. Eddie would go right now and put him in his will, if he could. But that was something for later.  “But I saw him sitting here and I didn’t see any adult with him and I just-”

“It’s fine,” Eddie said, cutting him off. There was something about Chris’ striped yellow shirt that was bothering him, especially next to the white tee that Buck was wearing beneath a salmon colored button-up. “I’m not gonna bite your face off for being concerned about him.”

“Still, I didn’t mean-”

“Chris said you’re a firefighter, too?”

“Uh, I- I was…” Buck said, hand rubbing at his leg. He stopped the moment he caught Eddie watching the motion. “Station One Eighteen. You?”

“Station Six,” Eddie answered. “Though I almost went with that one.”

That was the point of divergence, as far as he could tell. Bobby wasn’t as convincing this time around, wasn’t able to lure him onto the team. Oh, this past year sucked without Buck - and therefore, Carla - in their lives. Props to the Eddie-that-was for managing to get through it without collapsing under the weight.

Chris, losing interest in their conversation, turned and stood on the bench to look over the pier’s railing. Buck’s hand immediately went to grip the back of his shirt to stabilize him, and when he realized what he’d just done, he cringed and gave Eddie a wide-eyed look. He relaxed when he realized that Eddie wasn’t going to get after him for the simple act of ensuring his (their) kid’s safety.

“Why, uh. Why didn’t you go with the One Eighteen?” Buck asked, his fingers gripping tight to Chris’ shirt. The boy himself didn’t pay him any mind as he happily munched on his cotton candy and looked over the ocean.

“Dunno,” Eddie admitted with a shrug. “Went left instead of right, I suppose.”

“Well, there’s a spot open there now, if-”

“Hey, Dad?” Chris’s voice interrupted them. “Where did all the water go?”

The words had Eddie’s stomach dropping out as he realized exactly what his instincts had been trying to warn him about. Far, far too late, the warning siren began to blare. Buck grabbed Chris and threw him over his shoulder as he and Eddie sprinted for some semblance of cover, shouting for people to get off the pier, for all the good that it would do.

Where was the fire engine? Eddie remembered that Buck and Chris took refuge on top of the 136’s engine, so where was it? He was so busy searching for it that he almost missed when they came to a baseball toss stand, where Buck gently lowered Chris behind the flimsy safety of plywood and cloth. Then he looked at Eddie, and Eddie looked at him, before diving behind it themselves.

And then the wave hit.

It was a solid wall, crashing and tearing through everything in its path. Eddie almost couldn’t hear anything over the rushing and the roaring of the water about to kill them, but he just about made out the sound of wood splintering. The game stand they were sheltering in did next to nothing, and they were soon swept away with the rest of the flotsam.

There was no chance of staying together, though Eddie tried. He reached for Chris’ hand a mere moment before they were all three torn away from each other. The water tossed him about, smashed him into debris and smashed debris into him. He went ass over teakettle, his only reference for “up” the faint light of the California sun trying its best to reach through the ocean waters.

When Eddie finally managed to break the surface, finally managed to take a gulp of air instead of sea, he was struck by how loud it was. It had been silent beneath the water, but now he was hit by another wave, this one of pure noise. The roaring of the water that was still dragging him along and the wailing of car alarms.

And the shouting.

“Chris! Chris!” That was Buck. When Eddie grabbed hold of a tree and could take in his surroundings, he spotted Buck hanging onto a string of lights. “Eddie!”

But where was-?

“Daddy!” Chris was downstream from them both, arms wrapped around a light post.

“I’m right here!” Eddie shouted, loud as he could. Buck twisted around to see him. “Christopher, mijo, hold on! I’m coming!”

“We’re coming!” Buck added. With barely a glance Eddie’s way, he let go of the lights and allowed the water to drag him on, and propelled himself with his own strokes when that didn’t get him to the boy fast enough.

Eddie himself was not far behind, pushing off the tree and kicking himself forward. Buck got to Chris just before him, and he watched them reach out for each other, only for their fingertips to just brush as the water carried Buck past. Eddie, just a moment after him, managed to grab his kid’s hand. His kid, who immediately let go of the post to wrap himself around Eddie like an octopus.
