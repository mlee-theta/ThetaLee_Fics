Now

She was so damn small.

Buck knew he wasn’t supposed to be here, Bobby had made that perfectly clear when he stopped him from following the doctors through those double doors before. But he just couldn’t help himself, just couldn’t let himself forget about the tiny little bundle that he carried down the stairs. Sprinting like his life depended on it. Sprinting because her life depended on it. 

He should have listened to his captain, but Buck was only human. In the last not-quite five months of being a firefighter, he hadn’t crossed this line before. But this time? This time it had been a baby, so small and vulnerable in his arms. A tiny little life that had been entrusted to him, and he needed to know, couldn’t live with himself if he didn’t check.

The nurses had been wary, but people always talked about how charming Buck was. All it took was a smile, his firefighter’s uniform, and a comment that he was the one who brought her in. They melted. He wasn’t allowed to actually hold her again, and the nurses made sure he was aware that he was only to see her through a window, but it was enough.

She was somehow even tinier looking now than when he first saw her, hooked up to wires and tubes that he barely even knew the function of. So very tiny, and he could not understand for the life of him how-

“I don’t care how much you beg and pout, we are not taking it home with us.”

Buck rolled his eyes but didn’t bother to turn around.

“How did you know I was here?”

“Because I’m not an idiot.” Danny, his older brother. The best older brother. “I heard you got into it with a cop.”

“I… Might have.” Buck ducked his head. He wasn’t exactly proud of it, even if he would do it again. “The mom stuffed her down a pipe! She didn’t des-”

He went silent when Danny’s hand fell on his shoulder, heavy and firm.

“We’re not in the business of deciding who deserves anything,” he said. “We just help the people that need help.”

“Yeah, yeah,” Buck grumbled, suitably chastised. Though he was almost certain that putting them both - the mother and the child she tried to kill - on the same bus was against the regs, somehow.

He spotted Danny’s reflection in the glass, a reflection of a face so very similar to his own, rolling its eyes as the man shoved at his shoulder.

“She shoved her down a pipe?”

“Yeah!” Buck was still so pissed off about it. “Who even does that!?”

His brother let out a hum, one that Buck chose to assume was in agreement with his perfectly reasonable outrage. There were so many steps that the mom took to get to that point, so many better choices she could’ve made that didn’t end with them having to rescue a newborn baby from the plumbing.

It was Los Angeles! Most of those options, she didn’t even have to tell her parents!

“Some people are just shitty parents,” Danny said, in the arctic tone that only made an appearance when they brushed along certain topics. “Did anyone call it in?”

Buck blinked.

“Call it in?”

“We’re mandatory reporters, Buck,” his brother said, sounding more than a little exasperated with him for having to spell it out. “She stuffed her kid down a pipe to die. Did anyone call CPS?”

“Oh. I- I don’t know.” 

That seemed like the kind of thing Bobby was supposed to take care of, as the captain. Or maybe Hen and Chimney, since they were the paramedics. But Buck got the impression that both Bobby and Hen were on the cop’s side of things, sympathizing with the teen mom and seeing her as a victim. And as far as he was aware, Chimney hadn’t done it either.

“You should probably get on that, then,” Danny said, and then shoved at his shoulder again. “C’mon, the nurse over there has been making pointed glances at the clock since I arrived. I think your time’s up.”

“Yeah, okay.” Buck let out a sigh, pressed his palm against the glass before tearing himself away with great reluctance. She was so very tiny. “Y’know, I don’t live with you. I don’t need your permission to bring anything home with me.”

“You live in a frat house-”

“It’s not a frat house!”

“A frat house,” his brother insisted. “No social worker with a working brain would let you bring a newborn home to that. They wouldn’t even let you keep a pet gerbil.”

“It’s not that bad.”

“Yes it is.”

“No it isn’t.”

“Yes it is.”

“No it-” Buck cut himself off with a huff. “I’m hungry, buy me food.”

“I know we taught you manners,” Danny scolded as he led the way from the NICU. “And I also know that Nash feeds you over there.”

“Okay, seriously.” Buck followed along, resisting the urge to turn back. If he turned back, he would try to stay. And if he tried to stay, Danny would physically drag him out. “Who d’ya have spying on me?”

“I wouldn’t call it spying.”

“Fine, informant. Who’s your informant at the station?”

“I ain’t gonna tell ya. Now move your ass if you want to be fed. Slowpokes don’t get Happy Meals.”

Then

Daniel was the one who named the baby.

Their parents had sat him and Maddie down, explaining the hows and the whys. In as much detail as children their age needed to know, at least. Daniel didn’t care much for the specifics, too excited at the prospect of being an older brother. Maddie hadn’t quite shared his feelings at the start, but then again, her world was not so limited as his own. She had other things to be excited about, though Daniel didn’t know what could possibly be more exciting than this.

A baby brother, made just for him. Mom said so.

One day, Maddie showed up in his room with the Complete Book of Baby Names: Traditional and Modern. She had more freedom to do other things, so she didn’t spend nearly as much time going through them all as Daniel did. And Daniel spent almost all his time going through the book, determined to find the perfect name. 

He settled on Evan, although he wasn’t entirely certain why. It was how it sounded, maybe, and how it was spelled. According to the book, it was just a form of John, but John was a boring name. He couldn’t give his new brother a boring name. Evan wasn’t a boring name, he didn’t think. And it wasn’t a weird name, like Maddie. People would ask, sometimes, what Maddie was short for. But it wasn’t short for anything, that was just her name.

His brother needed a middle name, too, Daniel knew. He liked Evan, but he couldn’t be just Evan. This one wasn’t found in the Complete Book of Baby Names. Not that it wasn’t in there - it was! - but that wasn’t where he found it.

Mom was at his bedside, reading to him from a different book - Winnie-the-Pooh - and that’s where Daniel got the name. Christopher. He liked the sound of it so much that he almost changed his mind about his brother’s first name. Almost. But he stuck with Evan, because he’d already started calling him that in his head.

Evan Christopher.

Daniel liked the way they sounded together.

His parents didn’t argue when he told him what the baby’s name was going to be. They had laughed and agreed, and that was that. Later, after the years took the shine away from everything, he’d wonder if they would’ve bothered naming the baby at all if he hadn’t done the job for them.

Now

Sometimes, the calls that they responded to could get weird. Like, really, really weird. If Buck hadn’t spent years listening to Danny’s work stories from before they both moved, he would’ve thought that Los Angeles was simply like that. And to be fair, this city did seem to have more than its fair share of out-there emergencies. But it wasn’t like the city had an exclusive claim to them, either.

This call was pretty… Unique. Buck was gonna go with unique.

It was clear from dispatch that their caller - barely able to explain anything on account of being choked by their snake - wouldn’t be able to answer the door, so they didn’t bother knocking. They simply busted it open with the portable ram, an always reliable skeleton key, and rushed inside. The house wasn’t small, and there were several doors lining the front hall, which was not ideal when they didn’t even know where their caller was.

Bobby ordered them to check all the rooms and so they did, throwing open the doors as they passed and calling out on occasion in the hopes that their vic would be able to respond. But one of the last rooms had them all stumbling to a stop and was what clinched this call as one of the weirder that Buck had been on so far.

Bobby let out a quiet “wow” which summed things up quite well.

The room was like a scaled-down version of the reptile house as the zoo. One where whoever designed it wasn’t actually taking into account the safety of visitors or exhibits. In the center of the room was a fake tree, with several different constrictor snakes clinging to the limbs. Along the walls on either side were tanks that held smaller snakes, but which absolutely deserved larger enclosures.

There were a pair of doorways on the other end from them, though Buck couldn’t immediately tell where they led.

“Oh my god.” Chimney looked petrified, staring wide-eyed at the snake-tree and white-knuckling the halligan he carried in. “Bobby, I can’t do snakes. They scare the crap out of me. That scene from Conan the Barbarian with the giant snake, it traumatized me for life.”

“What’s Conan?” Buck asked, scrunching his nose up.

“Conan the Barbarian!” Chimney’s offense at Buck’s ignorance seemed to overpower his fear, at least temporarily. “Arnold Schwarzenegger? Nineteen Eighty-Two?”

“Sorry, dude,” Buck said with an apologetic shrug, leaning down to peer into one of the tanks containing either a king or coral snake. How did that rhyme go? Red on black… “I’ve got nothin’.”

His parents hadn’t exactly encouraged them to hang out in the living room to watch movies from before he was born. They also hadn’t encouraged them to do it for movies made after he was born, either. It wasn’t until Danny and their sister, Maddie, were able to take him to the theater that he actually got to see them on the regular. And by the time he was no longer living with his parents, he wasn’t exactly interested in old classics.

“Guys, in here!” Bobby called out, having crossed the room while they bickered. “Back here guys, now.”

Buck and the others hurried to him, finding themselves in the bedroom. On the other side of the bed, clutching at the ginormous snake wrapped around her throat, was their vic. Bobby immediately dropped to his knees beside her and wrapped his hands around the neck of the animal, straining with all his might to unwind it. The others stood back, frozen by either fear (Chimney) or uncertainty (Hen and Buck).

“We’ve got to help her get some air, guys,” Bobby told them, tugging on gloves to help with his grip.

“It’s no use,” Hen said, “That thing is like, ten feet long. Its constriction strength is fifty pounds per square inch.”

“Oh my god!” Chimney snapped, hovering in the doorway with the halligan held forth like a sword. “I’m gonna start calling you Snake-ipedia. Stop it!”

“All I’m saying,” Hen shot back, “is that you’d stand a better chance of tearing down a cement wall with your bare hands.”

“Why don’t I just punch it in its face?” Buck offered.

“You can’t punch it in the face,” Bobby said. He somehow managed to sound almost exactly like Danny in that moment, even though the two had never met. “It’s not some guy at an El Torito happy hour.”

Well, excuse Buck for trying to come up with a possible solution. Could the other two say the same? No, no they-

“Look, I have Dilaudid,” Hen said, already unzipping her medical bag. “We can inject the snake. It’ll pass right out.”

Nevermind.

“How much time is that gonna take?” Bobby asked, still pulling. It was apparently rhetorical, because he answered himself a moment later. “Minutes, and we don’t have any to spare. We’re gonna have to put it down.”

“Kill it!” Chimney very clearly agreed with the idea.

“No!” Hen protested. “No, no! It’s a snake, it’s doing what nature intended for it to do. Nobody told this fool to bring a snake into her house!”

Even while being strangled to death by her own pet, the woman they were trying to save managed to look offended by Hen’s statement.

“I’ll make a donation to PETA for you,” Bobby retorted. “Crap, it’s getting really tight.”

Okay, yeah, this had gone on long enough. The entire time they were bickering, the lady’s face had gotten redder and redder. She needed to be freed, Bobby had already decided the snake had to die, and Buck?

Buck had an ax.

“Stand back,” he told the others, hefting it up. “We don’t have time for this.”

The others cried out for him to stop as he brought the ax down.

“You’re the man behind for the rest of the day,” the captain told him.

“Because hopefully this way,” Bobby said, already turning on his heel, “you’ll learn to think before you start swinging.”

“Tough break,” Hen said, patting his shoulder as she and a snickering Chimney went by. 

This wasn’t the first time that Buck had been stuck as the man behind on calls. He was the probie, and the probie tended to be stuck with the short-end of the stick. Buck was just lucky that Bobby wasn’t one of the captains who almost never let the probies out on calls.

He was pretty sure taking one of the trucks out without permission was definitely something that the captain wouldn’t look kindly on.

Then

“Okay, Evan, this is it,” Maddie said, undoing the last screw keeping the training wheel attached to Daniel’s old bike. She set it and the wheel aside, with the other wheel and screws and nuts and bolts. “You ready?”

“Ready,” Evan said, placing the helmet firmly on his head.

Daniel, hovering anxiously nearby, could only be thankful that at least one of them was ready. Because he definitely wasn’t, no sir. But if his baby brother was ready to learn how to ride a bike without training wheels, then Daniel was not going to stop him. Fret the entire time, maybe, but not stop him.

It was a bit rough, at the start. Evan had issues balancing on the bike, and then kept falling over before he even managed to get a foot forward. Each time he fell, each time he let out a pained yelp, Daniel’s stomach twisted. Maddie looked to be doing better than him, but only just.

“Are you sure you don’t want to go back to training wheels?” Daniel asked, trying to keep the anxiousness out of his words. “There’s nothing wrong with that.”

“No!” Evan said, pushing the bike upright again. “I’m a big kid, I don’t need them!”

“Okay, okay,” Maddie broke in, helping him get back on.

This time, she and Daniel pushed the bike along as Evan pedaled, which helped it get going.

“You’re doing great, Ev!” Daniel said, trying to encourage his brother.

“Just get a feel for it,” Maddie added.

“But you’re not going to let go,” Evan said, the statement coming out more like an uncertain question.

“We’re not gonna let go,” Maddie assured him, before meeting Daniel’s eyes and doing just that. And, reluctantly, Daniel followed his sister’s example.

“You let go!” Evan immediately accused, already several feet away from where they stood and watched.

“But you’re doing it!” Daniel shouted back.

“I’m doing it!” Evan must’ve just realized it, because he sounded excited now. “I’m doing it!”

Except he was quickly approaching the intersection, and Maddie realized a small detail that they had overlooked at this point.

“Oh God,” she said, “we forgot turning.”

She and Daniel shared a panicked look.

“Turn the wheel, Evan, and lean a little!” Maddie shouted.

“Turn and lean!” Daniel was hopping in place as he yelled. “Turn and lean! Turn and- Yes!”

“Evan!” Maddie screamed at the same time as Daniel, the pair of them rushing to their brother’s side. The knee of his jeans was torn, and he was bleeding.

“What did you do to him!?” was what Mom shouted.

“You’re living a lie!” the captain finally snapped. And then, a bit calmer, “You let her manipulate you. You pretend to be somebody you’re not, making up stories to satisfy her hero fetish. What is that?”

“You know what? Maybe I wouldn’t have to make up stories if, every once in a while, you let me take point on something,” Chimney said. “I just have to operate the winch, right? And the Maneuver? I can do the Maneuver.”

This would not have been an argument at Danny’s old station, Buck didn’t think. Back in Texas, there were paramedics and there were firefighters, separate but equal. It had actually taken some getting used to, finding out that the paramedics here were both. So he hadn’t actually put any thought to the fact that Chimney never really did the exciting stuff on calls.

“I know you can, that’s not the point,” Bobby told Chimney. “The point is that your entire relationship is a lie. Come on, you asked me to make the two of you dinner so you could pass it off as your own. She isn’t in love with you, she’s in love with the fantasy version of you.”

That was extremely brutal and blunt, Buck thought, as he tried - and failed - to catch Hen’s eye across the table. Bobby was from Minnesota, the Midwest. Weren’t Midwesterners all supposed to be big on politeness? Apparently, there was a limit to it. Or a limit to Bobby’s, at least.

“You know what? I feel bad for your wife, I really do,” Chimney said, turning his back on the captain and stomping away. “It must be tough, living with someone who thinks he’s right all the time.”

“Chim,” Hen tried, but the man shook his head at her as he headed for the stairs.

“I’m getting some air!”

There was tension left in the wake of Chimney’s departure. Everyone moved slowly and carefully, like it risked breaking the fragile peace and bringing forth yet more shouting if they didn’t. It was discomforting in its familiarity to the atmosphere he grew up with in Hershey, following every blow-up argument between his siblings and his parents.

“Done setting the table?” Bobby asked, gaze fixed on where Chimney had left.

“Uh, almost,” Buck said, hurriedly setting down a fork. Across from him, Hen did the same.

“Finish up,” the captain said, turning back to their dinner. “And then go round up the others.”

Daniel could swear that traffic accidents made up at least half of the calls they responded to. His brother might have been able to rattle off the actual statistic for it, maybe. It depended on if Buck’s attention was caught on anything even tangentially related enough to drag him down the rabbit hole. Like the snakes, not too long ago. The text chain between them had been filled with an awful lot of seemingly random snake facts for about a week.

“Car was driving erratically,” Mehta briefed them all on the ride to the scene. “Swerved to avoid something on the road, nearly drove into a K-rail, and got rear ended by a work truck. Dispatch reports a bunch of smaller accidents right behind them.”

Delightful, Daniel sarcastically thought as he got them onto the correct on-ramp and then proceeded to go the wrong way down the empty lanes to avoid the backed-up traffic. 

It was an utter mess when they got there, with pieces of cars littering the pavement. Most of them seemed to be minor, though a couple were smashed into each other. The ones of biggest concern were at the epicenter, a blue car crumpled in the front by the concrete barrier and in the back by the work truck. The work truck which had been carrying rebar rods. One of them had been dislodged by the impact and was now spearing the smaller vehicle.

Daniel put the truck into park, hopped out, and jogged over to check on the driver of the car with Mehta while the others fanned out to start triaging. The driver, an asian man, was miraculously alive, awake, and aware. Miraculously, because the rod of rebar was sticking out of his head. His eyes were darting around in a panic.

“Okay, um. Don’t move,” Mehta said, unnecessarily. “We’ll have you out of this. Mendoza! Huff!”

“No!” the driver cried out, trying to shake his head. He cried out again when Daniel reached in to hold him still. Maybe Mehta’s instructions had been necessary. “I want Bobby. Captain Nash. Get Captain Nash.”

Did he mean Buck’s Captain Nash?

“Sir,” Mehta said, “can you tell us your name?”

“Howard Han.” There was a Han that Buck worked with. It was looking very likely that this was that Han. “I want Bobby.”

Daniel shared a look with Mehta, and then stepped away from the car to pull out his phone. He’d had Nash’s number saved in his contacts since shortly after Buck started at the 118. It was mostly a just-in-case sort of thing, so that he wouldn’t ignore it and let it ring through if Nash called him about his brother.

This had never been in the cards.

“Hello,” Nash answered after only a couple rings.

“Man, do I wish there was a better reason for us to finally be talking.”

“Who is this?”

“Daniel Buckley,” he said. Mehta had stepped away too and was currently talking to dispatch on the radio; he gave Daniel a nod. “You’re about to get called out. It’s one of yours. Howard Han. He’s asking for you.”

He glanced back to the car and its driver, at the rebar sticking out of the man’s head, and added,

“It’s not good.”

It was not often that the captain was called directly about an emergency.

Nor was it often that the emergency they were responding to involved one of theirs.

“Captain Nash,” he greeted, nodding to Buck where he stood a bit behind the captain with Hen.

“Thanks for calling,” Bobby said, shaking Danny’s hand.

“Your guy’s over there.” Danny jerked his thumb over his shoulder. Buck followed the direction with his eyes, but all he could see was the back of a work truck. “He got upset every time one of us tried to treat him, kept asking for you. It’s, uh.” Danny paused and his eyes flickered over to Buck and Hen. “It ain’t pretty.”

“Hang back, guys,” Bobby ordered the pair of them, before following Danny.

“It’s worse than just being in a wreck.” Buck was mostly thinking aloud as he watched Bobby walk past the work truck and then kneel down. Presumably to talk to Chimney. “I know that tone, he didn’t want to come out and say it in front of us.”

“Buckley,” Hen said, clearly having seen the name on the back of Danny’s coat. She looked as worried as he felt. “Any relation?”

“That’s Danny, my brother.”

“How did I not know your brother worked at the One Thirty-three?”

Buck shrugged, because it was a good question with a very simple answer: it had just never come up, somehow. He was about to tell her exactly that when Bobby returned, the grave expression on his face confirming Buck’s thoughts regarding his brother’s understatement.

“Go get your kit and the tools,” the captain ordered.

“Cap,” Hen said, “how bad is it?”

The answer was apparently “very bad,” or so Bobby explained to them as they hurriedly grabbed what they needed. Buck was still trying to wrap his mind around the very idea of a piece of rebar going through a man’s head without killing them, without them even knowing about it. Actually seeing Chimney like that was not something Buck thought he could’ve ever been prepared for.

But Bobby told them they had to be cool about it, so he was trying.

“Airways sound good,” Hen said, taking off the stethoscope. She’d climbed into the front passenger seat to assess Chimney. 

Buck was half-laying on top of the crumpled hood. Well, actually, he was on the windshield that had been removed for easier access to the interior, but the windshield was on top of the hood, so it was the same thing.

“How’s your pain level?” Bobby asked, once again kneeling by the driver’s side. Danny was standing right beside him, across from Buck.

“Not much pain,” Chimney answered, and Buck still wasn’t sure how that was possible. Sure, Hen said the brain itself couldn’t feel pain, but the rest of his head should’ve been able to. Bobby was probably - definitely - right that Chimney was in shock, but holy shit. “I don’t think there’s any spinal damage. I can feel my legs, but when I try to move my head-”

“Don’t!” they all cried out together, instinctively jerking forward to stop him from even trying. The last thing Chimney needed to do was churn butter inside his own skull.

“Stay still,” Bobby told him.

“What’s going on?” Chimney asked, eyes darting between each of them. “Wh- Why aren’t you guys cutting me out of here?”

“It’s complicated.”

“Why?”

“You’ve been injured, Chim.”

Buck saw Danny furrow his brow before nodding and letting out a silent, “Oh.”

“How bad is it?” Chimney asked.

“It’s not good.” Buck and Danny said it at the exact same time. But somehow, it was only Buck that got the scolding look from Hen.

“Well, I want to see it.”

“Picture a unicorn,” Danny said, and this time he was on the receiving end of Hen’s glower. Not that he paid her any attention. “Just… Less horse, more blood.”

“A unicorn!?” Chimney’s eyes went wide. “Bobby, show me.”

The captain sighed, pulled out his phone, and held it up as he took a short video showing  how the man looked at that moment. Then he turned it around, so the screen faced Chimney, and let the man see for himself.

“Holy crap,” Chimney whispered. “How come I’m not dead?”

“‘Cause you’re the luckiest and the unluckiest son of a bitch on the face of the earth,” was Bobby’s answer. “At the same time.”

“I can’t even feel this,” Chimney said. “Please get me out of here before I start to.”

“We’re working on it,” Bobby assured him. “Normally, I’d be consulting my smartest EMT, but he’s currently got rebar stuck in his head.”

The captain stood up and jerked his head for everyone to follow him a few feet away. Hen pulled herself out of the car and rounded it to the other side; Buck simply clambered over the hood. Danny rolled his eyes as he joined them, along with Captain Mehta.

“This is a new one for me,” Danny said. Mehta nodded in agreement.

“We can’t get him out of that,” Hen told them. “Not here. It’s simply too dangerous, too many things could go wrong. It’s safer to extract him at the ER with the doctors right there.”

“Then we’ll have to take him to the hospital in the car,” Bobby said, looking over the scene again. “Which means we have to get that truck out of the way, stabilize him and the rebar…”

“I’ll call for a flatbed tow,” Mehta said, grabbing for his radio.

“C’mon,” Danny said, slapping Buck on the arm to get his attention and heading for the truck. Buck hurried in his wake. “Mendoza! Get your ass over here with the others!”

The most difficult part of moving the work truck wasn’t actually moving it. With a half-dozen buff firefighters, manhandling it backwards and then to the side of the road was actually rather easy. No, the most difficult part was moving it without jarring Chimney’s car. Which meant they had to ease the two vehicles apart, holding their breath and keeping an eye on the part of the rebar sticking out of the car roof.

Once the work truck was out of the way, Buck worked with Hen and Bobby to get the rebar and Chimney stabilized while Danny and the others cleared the way for the tow truck to get where it needed. By the time it arrived, they’d used ropes and straps and even torn-out seatbelts to insure that neither Chimney nor the rebar could move a single centimeter.

Bobby was going to ride in the passenger seat with him, just in case.

“Hey, Buck,” Chimney said as the car was drawn onto the bed of the tow truck. “How’s the car? Is it a total loss?”

Buck looked from one end of the car to the other. He looked at the way both ends were crumpled, the front bumper hanging off and the back bumper practically fused into the backseat. He looked at the missing door on the driver’s side, at the missing front windshield and the shattered back one. He looked at the rod that was speared through the roof.

There was no salvaging it.

“Nah, man,” Buck lied. “You… It’ll buff out.”

He didn’t think Chimney believed him one bit.

Then

Evan was crying.

That day was Daniel’s first day back home. His first day of freedom from the hospital after so, so very long. Everyone had been so excited for him, all the nurses and the doctors and the other patients. There was a bell that they let him ring, and he rang it as loud as he could.

When he got home, there’d been a party. All the neighbors were there, just as excited for Daniel to be home as he was. Mom cried a lot, both when he rang the bell and when Dad helped him get through the front door. Everyone was happy and excited for Daniel to be home, but there was something…

Evan was still so very small. Not as tiny as the first time Daniel had seen him, through a glass window a couple weeks after his birth, when Daniel managed to beg and wheedle his way into a visit. The baby had grown, but he was still so small, with little socks and little shirts and little pants.

Evan wasn’t at the party welcoming Daniel home. Or, he was, but he was tucked away in another room. The nursery, which Daniel hadn’t seen since it was the guest room. Occasionally, there’d be a piercing wail that would interrupt everyone. A wail that would continue until Mom let out a sigh and went to see what was wrong.

She was never gone for long.

Finally, Maddie ended up bringing the baby out with them, awkwardly carrying him in her arms as best as she could. Daniel was quick to have her sit next to him on the couch, so he could coo over his baby brother. Evan seemed much happier like this, because he didn’t cry again.

But that was earlier in the day. It was the middle of night, now, and Daniel was having trouble falling asleep in his room. A room he hadn’t been in for so long. It was familiar but unfamiliar at the same time. He’d gotten used to all the various noises of the hospital, quiet though it may have been at night. The sounds of nurses doing their rounds, of quiet chatter down the hall, of machines keeping time.

And he wasn’t the only one having trouble sleeping, because Evan was crying.

Daniel threw back his sheets and got to his feet, taking a moment to dig his bare toes into the carpet of his room. Carefully and quietly, he padded out into the hall and to the door of the nursery. It swung open easily and the crying got louder, the increase in volume making Daniel wince as he rushed to close the door again.

When he got to the side of the crib, the baby’s face was scrunched up and red from his crying. Daniel reached down and traced a finger along the spots above Evan’s eye. Mom told everyone who asked that it was a birthmark and that the blemishes would hopefully fade as he got older. Daniel hoped the opposite, hoped the spots would stay.

They looked neat.

The crying stuttered to a stop at his touch. Eyes, blue and shiny, blinked up at him, bright with the reflection of the light from the streetlamps outside.

“Hi, Evan,” Daniel whispered. “Why are you crying?”

“It’s probably his diaper,” Dad said, startling Daniel with his suddenness and then blinding him momentarily when he hit the switch for the light. He hadn’t heard him coming down the hall or opening the door.

“Oh.” Daniel didn’t know how to change a diaper.

“What are you doing awake, champ?” Dad asked, crossing the distance to the crib. He reached down and picked Evan up, the baby letting out a little gurgling cry.

“Couldn’t sleep,” Daniel answered. “Can- Can I help?”

“Help?” Dad blinked down at him, like he didn’t understand. “Uh, sure. Grab a diaper from over there.”

Daniel spotted the bag and dug a diaper out, carrying over to where Dad had Evan laying on the changing table.

Changing a diaper didn’t look that hard, though it was just wet. Daniel wasn’t sure if he wanted to change a poopy diaper, but he would if he had to. Evan had been crying for a while before Daniel got up to check on him. If he’d known what was wrong sooner, if he’d known how to make it better, then Evan wouldn’t have had to cry for so long.

When Dad was done, he set Evan back down in the crib. Then he turned off the light and escorted Daniel back to his room, where he tucked him in again. But the moment Dad was gone, Daniel was once more slipping out from under the covers.

Evan was still awake when he crept back into the nursery, being careful to stay silent. The baby made a cooing noise at the sight of him, and it warmed something in Daniel’s chest.

“Mom said you were made for me,” he said, reaching over the crib railing to once again run his finger over Evan’s birthmark. “That means I’ll take care of you.”

It wasn’t a pinky promise like the ones he made with Maddie, but Daniel meant it with every fiber of his young being.

Now

Fourteen hours after watching Chimney being wheeled beyond the glass doors of the emergency room, Buck was losing his mind. They hadn’t been sent to too many scenes overnight or that morning, and the ones they’d responded to had been minor at most. Which meant there’d been next to nothing to keep him distracted from the anxiety clawing up his throat. Nothing except the ability to bug Danny with too many texts, but his brother had been lucky enough to actually have calls that required his attention.

So now, at the end of his rope and desperately trying to resist the urge to literally start climbing the walls, Buck was pacing across the station’s loft. Hen was sitting at the dining table, filling in that day’s crossword puzzle and doing a remarkable impression of someone not bothered that there was no word yet on their friend.

“How long’s it take to pull a rod out of a guy’s skull, anyway?” Buck demanded of nobody in particular, making a face at his phone. Danny hadn’t responded to his last deluge of texts about a guy named Phineas Gage. To be fair, there’d been twenty of them sent in the last ten minutes, but still.

“You ever heard of that saying, it ain’t brain surgery? Well, this is,” Hen said. She sounded more tired than anything, with that extra special note of encroaching irritation that Buck tried very hard to learn to recognize as a kid. “What’s three across for when double in…?”

She trailed off as Bobby joined them.

“Just off the phone with the surgeon,” the captain told them.

“He’s alive?” Buck hurried to ask, and got a nod in return. Buck could almost feel that weight falling off his shoulders.

“He still have both eyes?” Hen asked, and got another nod. Phineas Gage, Buck now knew, lost one of his eyes. The left one. But that was because the rod that impaled his head had gone through it.

“Any… Any brain damage?” Buck asked. There had to have been some, there was simply no way there wasn’t any brain damage. But Chimney had been awake and aware right up until they were taking him to the ER, so…

“There were some complications when they took the rebar out,” Bobby said. Buck felt his heart drop. “He started hemorrhaging. They got it under control, but they put him in a medically induced coma. They said they’ll let us know if his condition changes.”

“You mean if he wakes up.”

“Or if he doesn’t,” Hen said. Hearing the words was akin to being dashed upon the rocks.

“Yeah,” was Bobby’s quiet agreement.

It was a relief, when the alarms sounded shortly after. A tractor trailer filled with people, baking in the California heat and screaming for help. The cops were already on the scene when the trucks and ambulance pulled up, cordoning off the area around where it was parked.

“Is it wrong I was stoked there was an emergency?” Buck asked Hen as he got out the tools needed to break into the trailer. “I was climbing the walls back at the house.”

“We all were, and yeah, it’s wrong,” she told him. “We’re not here to blow off steam. We’re here to help.”

As if the ability to do both of those things at the same time wasn’t one of the reasons Buck loved the job so much.

“Truck is registered out of Texas,” one of the cops told Bobby. The way Buck’s ears perked up at that was probably instinctive. “We’re still looking for the driver.”

“It’s got to be over a hundred degrees in there,” Bobby remarked. “Buck!”

The trailer doors were padlocked shut. A pair of real heavy-duty locks, too. But thankfully, as a firefighter, they had access to heavy-duty skeleton keys. In this case, power saws. It took a small bit of time, but before long, the locks were falling to the ground and Buck and Bobby were swinging the trailer doors open.

There were people inside. Buck knew that already, and yet it was still a shock to see. Most of them were collapsed on the trailer floor or against the walls, but there were some still standing. They were all sweating, clothes soaked through with it.

A pair of them, man and woman, hurried over and shoved a young boy into Buck’s arms, talking in rapid-fire Spanish as they did.

“[Help him, please,]” the father begged. “[He won’t wake up. He’s not breathing. Please.]”

“[I’ve got him,]” Buck assured the man, carrying the kid over to where a blanket had been laid out on the pavement by one of the others. “Hen!”

Behind him, Bobby was speaking fast into his radio, asking for more ambulances and a helicopter for medevacs. Buck barely paid attention to the captain, busy throwing aside his helmet and tugging off his gloves so he could assess the boy. Hen hurried over, dropping to her knees with her med kit.

“It’s at least four and a half hours to the Mexican border,” Hen said as she tugged on Nitrile gloves. “If that’s even where they started.”

The truck was registered in Texas, so it was probably at Ciudad Juarez that they piled into that trailer. That was a twelve-hour drive to Los Angeles if they crossed the border there, longer if it was at a more southern crossing. Buck had driven it once, with Danny, when they moved. He would’ve told Hen, except the boy was not breathing and so he had bigger priorities.

“Starting compressions,” Buck announced, already placing his palms together on the kid’s chest before Hen even gave him the go ahead. The little chest gave beneath his strength with every press of his arms.

“[Wake up, Javi,]” the father begged as he worked. “[Please, mijo, wake up. Wake up.]”

Hen got the vent bag in place and Buck paused in the motions as she pumped air into the kid’s mouth. The parents begged, Hen begged, and Buck held his breath while he waited for the kid - for Javi - to take one. And then, just as he was about to start compressions again, the kid started gasping and coughing.

“[Good job, Javi,]” Buck told the boy. The mom collapsed in relief against the father’s shoulder, both of them crying. To the parents, he said, “[He’s okay. He’ll be okay. My friend will check him over and someone will get you all water. Okay?]”

Hen gave him a curious frown, tilting her head in question. Buck just shrugged.

“[Thank you,]” the father said, reaching up to grab at his arm. “[Thank you so much.]”

Buck smiled and waved away the thanks, before hurrying to help with the other patients in the trailer. And there were a lot of them. As other ambulances from other stations started arriving, it became an assembly line. Get an unconscious man or woman out of the trailer to lay them out on the blankets that were being put down to protect them from the hot pavement. They did what they could to deal with the symptoms of heat exhaustion, giving out what bottles of water they had on hand and pressing cooling packs onto feverishly hot skin. 

Mostly, though, Buck found himself playing translator. Especially once the others started clocking onto him having actual conversations with the more awake patients. Then they started calling him over so he could fill in the role of telephone, relaying questions and answers.

“Hey, dick! Where the hell do you think you’re going!?” Hen suddenly shouted. Buck’s head whipped up in the middle of one of those conversations to see her tackling a guy on the other side of the yellow police tape. “Sick son of a bitch. Whole families could have died in there. Someone with a badge and a gun want to put this trash in custody!?”

One of the uniformed officers hurried to take it from there. Buck, at a more sedate pace, went over as well.

“That was badass,” he remarked. “But I thought we weren’t here to blow off steam.”

“You know what you can blow,” Hen spat at him with a glare, before stomping away.

Buck’s phone ringing in his pocket distracted him from the fact that his friend was very clearly not handling things any better than he was. It was Abby’s ringtone, the one he set after he turned down her offer of a date and they agreed to stick to talking on the phone. The sound of it automatically brought a smile to his face and he wanted to answer her, he did. Except they were at a scene, with people that needed help, and he did not want to even imagine Danny’s disappointment if he found out about him chatting on the phone instead of doing his job.

He did, however, call Abby back once they’d returned to the station and he took a quick shower. She picked up after a couple rings.

“Hey, sorry, we were at a call,” Buck told her, tucking himself away in a quiet corner from everyone else. Away from any prying eyes or ears that might be passing messages along to his brother. “We just got back to the station.”

“It’s fine, I should have- It’s fine.” She sounded tired. It piqued his concern.

“Everything okay?”

“Yeah,” Abby said. “It’s just my mom. She has Alzheimer’s, did I tell you that? I can’t remember if I did.”

“No, I- I didn’t know that. I’m sorry.” Even to Buck’s own ears, that sounded lame.

“Thanks. She’s just… Slipping, y’know? Getting worse, and I- I’m starting to feel like she might need more than just me,” she said. “More help. Professional help.”

“You mean, like a home?”

“Yeah.” There was a sigh on the other end of the line. “Just the thought of it paralyzes me with guilt. I mean, she’s my mom. Right?”

“Uh. Yeah, that’s rough.” Buck was well aware that his relationship with his mother was not the same as most people’s. It was very clearly not the same as the one Abby had with her mother. “Look, I- I don’t know a lot, I’m a bit of a dumbass. But I know that sometimes family, actual family, is the only thing that matters.”

There was a distinction there, one he wasn’t sure how to get across. His parents were family, but he hadn’t spoken to the two of them in years and years. Danny and Maddie, though, were family, and he’d do just about anything for them.

“Right, you’re right. Thank you.”

“You got it,” Buck said, and then settled in and got more comfortable. “Hey, uh, I’ve got time if you do. Anything else going on over there? Any crazy calls?”

Abby let out a hum.

“My brother’s visiting with his family…”

After shift, they - Buck and Bobby and Hen - headed for the hospital. They were in such a hurry that they didn’t bother changing into their civvies, simply grabbing their bags and going. It was probably to their advantage, though. Buck knew from experience that the nurses were more likely to be lenient with visitation rules if they showed up in uniform.

After they arrived at the hospital, Buck and Hen ended up having to wait in a waiting room just outside the ICU while Bobby talked to a nurse and a middle-aged Asian couple.

“Who’re they?” Buck asked her, jerking his thumb their way. “Chimney’s parents?”

“Anne and John Lee.” Hen explained. “They took him in after his mom died. So, they’re not his parents, but…” She shrugged. “They’re his parents.”

“I get that.”

Hen eyed him up, something considering in her expression.

“You’re a white boy from New England-”

“Pennsylvania is not New England, not even close.”

“Whatever.” She dismissed it with a flick of her wrist. “You’re fluent in Spanish?”

“I can also make a mean pozole,” Buck bragged. “I was born in Pennsylvania, but I went to high school in Texas. Danny and I had an apartment like, right by the border. We used to do day trips into Juarez. Danny hated whenever I went without him, though, and it was a pain in the ass to cross back, sometimes.”

“Danny and you.” Hen squinted her eyes at him behind her glasses. “Just Danny and you?”

“Just Danny and me,” he confirmed with a nod. 

Buck wasn’t sure if she would have pressed for more or not. She was curious, he could tell, and he knew he had a bad habit of not actually telling his coworkers things about his life. See: the fact that Hen had no clue his brother worked at the 133. Actually, she might not have even known he had a brother prior to meeting him in person. Woops. 

That definitely meant it wasn’t her tattling on him to his brother, though. So who was it?

Whether she was going to take the opportunity to press for more or not didn’t matter, however. Bobby finished up his conversation with the nurse and the Lees and came over, which meant the topic was gonna be shelved for the time being.

“They’re gonna let us see him,” the captain said. Hen straightened up at the words. “C’mon.”

The nurse he’d been talking to led them into the ICU, coming to a stop just outside one of the individual rooms. On the other side of the sliding glass door, Buck could see Chimney laying on the bed. There was a bandage wrapped around his head, an oxygen cannula under his nose, and a small line of stitches visible on his forehead where the rebar went through.

“He’s a tough nut,” the nurse said. “He’s gonna be in here for a few days. Intubation tube is out, obviously, but the catheter’s still in.”

There was no way that would be comfortable when he woke up.

“Can he hear us?” Buck asked.

“I always treat them like they can.” She pushed the door open. “Two minutes, then I’m kicking you all out.”

They filed into the room and the nurse closed the door behind them. Buck grabbed a chair that was off to the side and offered it to Hen, but she turned it down in favor of staying standing. She looked nervous.

“Hey, Chim.” Bobby was sitting in a chair on the other side of the bed. He grabbed Chimney’s hand in his own. “We’re all here for you.”

Buck saw Chimney’s fingers twitch and wrap around Bobby’s hand. Hen let out a shaky breath and sank down to sit on the bed by the man’s feet.

“Hey, so, I- I was thinking that you could do the Maneuver next time, instead of me. When you get back.” Buck ducked his head slightly at the looks that garnered from Hen and Bobby. “To motivate him to get better.”

“He can’t do the Maneuver,” Hen whispered.

“Sure he can,” he whispered back. “Chim, you can do the Maneuver, right?”

As they watched, the thumb of the hand that Bobby was holding slowly went up.

“Chim,” Hen said. “I tried to get ahold of your dad-” Buck scrunched up his face and looked at her. He’d assumed Chimney’s dad was gone, too, since the Lees took him in. “There’s a sixteen hour time difference with Seoul, so that might’ve been the problem.”

“But the Lees were here,” Bobby added. “And we’re here. So it’s fine.”

“So,” Danny said once he’d hung up.

“So,” Buck echoed. “Abby’s gone.”

“Oh no.” He put his phone down on the end table and picked up the book that was laying there. “How awful.”

Buck scowled, because he could at least pretend to be upset about this. But he let it go, because he was well aware that Danny apparently had strong opinions about his ex-girlfriend.

“I don’t think I want to stay at the craftsman anymore,” he said instead. Danny froze in the motion of opening the book, eyes darting over to him. “I’m kinda over all the partying, y’know?”

“Okay…”

“So could I… Could I move back here?”

“Yes!” Danny said it so suddenly and so loudly that it startled both of them into jumping. He cleared his throat, waved his hand in the air. “I- I mean, yes. Of course. Evan Christopher Buckley, you’re my baby brother. Mi casa es su casa. Of course you can move back here.”

“Okay, cool.”

“Cool.”

Buck sank against the arm of the couch and closed his eyes, and simply let himself be, before he had to get up and start ferrying all of his things back over from the craftsman and Abby’s apartment.

Then

The jeep was not new, but it shined like it was. Daniel had made sure, before any money was handed over, that it was in as close to perfect condition as it possibly could be. It was going to be ferrying important cargo, after all. It needed to be trustworthy.

The trunk door slammed shut, and his heart stammered with the sound.

“Is that the last of it?” he asked, doing his best to keep his nerves from showing in his voice.

“It is,” Evan confirmed, rounding the jeep to stand in front of him. “Not like I’m taking all that much, just a few bags. I won’t be gone long, just a few months.”

“I know,” Daniel said. “Where are you heading first?”

“I don’t know,” his brother said, ducking his head and digging the toe of his shoe into the pavement of the parking lot. “I’m thinking I’ll go see Maddie first.”

That was not the original first destination, Daniel knew. The itinerary of this road trip had been planned out over the past couple of months, a pair of heads bent over a map of the United States and arguing over which way to go, what stops to make. Evan was not supposed to be going back to Hershey first thing, and he was especially not supposed to be going alone.

“Wherever you go,” Alma said, from where she stood beside them, “you had better call.”

“Every night,” Daniel agreed, ignoring the melodramatic groan his brother let out. “I don’t care where you are or what you’re doing. You call me and let me know you’re alive.”

“Yeah, yeah,” Evan said, acting all the world like they were asking so much of him. But he was smiling the entire time. “I’ll miss you both, too.”

Daniel opened his mouth, a witty retort at the ready. But it caught in his throat, choking him on the words. Finally, he just threw his arms around his baby brother and brought him in close, squeezing as tight as he could. Evan immediately returned the hug, squeezing just as tight.

“Don’t kill him,” Evan whispered.

“I make no promises.”

“Danny…”

“Fine,” he said, letting go. “I won’t kill him, I won’t touch him. But I’m not going to be nice if I see him.”

“I wouldn’t want you to be.” Evan leaned over and hugged Alma as well. “Make sure he eats?”

“Of course,” she agreed, both of them ignoring Daniel’s empty protests.

There was another round of hugs, and then Evan was climbing behind the wheel of the jeep. Daniel and Alma watched and waved as he backed out of the parking spot, turned onto the road heading for the highway, and quickly went out of sight. Daniel probably would have kept standing there, in the middle of that parking lot, if not for Alma tugging on his arm to follow her.

For the first time in his life, Daniel was going to be alone.

Now

“Buck!”

Hen’s voice had him set down the barbell he was lifting and look up, spotting the paramedic making her way between the trucks towards him. He got up off the bench and grabbed his towel to wipe the sweat from his face.

“Yeah?”

“There’s a woman here to-”

Buck cut her off with a groan.

“Another one?”

“I don’t know,” Hen said. “I don’t think so. She doesn’t seem upset or angry like the others. She’s not throwing rocks at us, at least.”

The police, through MySpace, had sent messages to the women that the catfisher had been talking to. That had seemed to stop the flow of jilted would-be lovers flitting in and out of his life to harass and assault him. He’d hoped that would be the last he’d have to deal with it, but apparently not.

“Where is she?”

“Bay doors.” Hen jerked her thumb over her shoulder. “She said she’d wait out there.”

“If I get slapped again…”

“I’ll have Athena at the ready.”

Tossing the towel over his shoulder, Buck started walking for the bay doors. He didn’t spot the woman at first, not until he was at the doors and peering outside. She was leaning against the wall, arms crossed, but she straightened up when she spotted him.

It was not one of the catfishing victims. He wished it was one of the catfishing victims.

“Hey, Evan,” she said, awkwardly smiling.

“Shannon,” Buck bit out. She flinched at his unfriendly and unwelcoming tone. “What are you doing here?”

“I saw you on the news the other month,” she said. “The roller coaster? I was going to come sooner, but… Well, I wasn’t sure if you’d want to see me.”

He didn’t. He really, really didn’t. There were only a handful of people he wanted to see less. In fact, her being here meant one of them mightn’t be that far behind.

“Is he here, too?” he asked, dreading the answer.

“No,” Shannon said. It did not make Buck feel better, because a part of him still wanted so desperately… “He’s still in El Paso. I moved up here when Mom got sick again.”

Buck blinked, not comprehending at first. And then, when the words sunk in, he found himself laughing.

“You left him?” He shook his head, laughed again.. “Serves him right.”

“Look,” she said. “Evan-”

“Why did you come here, Shannon?” he asked, cutting her off.

“To see you?” It came out as a question. “It’s- I haven’t seen you since that Christmas, a couple years after you left. How- How are you?”

“I was doing fine before you came here,” was Buck’s blunt answer. Somewhere behind him was a whispered “ damn” and he realized that Hen had followed him. Probably in case he needed back-up. “You were right, I didn’t want to see you.”

“Oh,” was all Shannon said, and all she had time to say, before Buck was turning around and walking back into the station.

Hen was on his heels a few moments later, probably having lingered behind to make sure Shannon left okay. Buck was expecting it when, as he sat down on the bench in the station’s gym, she loomed over him and asked,

“An ex?”

“My ex’s ex,” he said. “Apparently.”

“Not a good break up,” she guessed.

“No,” Buck agreed. “It wasn’t,”

Then

There was knocking at the door, and Daniel did not want to deal with it.

Yesterday, Evan had driven off in his jeep on the start of his gap year road trip. Afterwards, Daniel drifted through the day until it was time for his shift at the station. It was a welcome distraction, even if it seemed that the EMS gods decided to punish him for thinking it. They’d been busy all night, meaning he was exhausted when he got back home.

But now, instead of being able to pull the covers over his head, he had to answer the damn door. Sure, he could simply ignore them, but from the way they were frantically knocking, he wasn’t so sure they’d stop if he didn’t see who it was.

Daniel  threw back the covers, got to his feet, and stomped through the tiny apartment. Yanking open the door, he had to squeeze the doorknob with all his might, just so he didn’t immediately grab the baseball bat sitting in the corner.

“What,” he snarled, “do you want?”

“Is Evan here?” Eddie fucking Diaz, the last person Daniel wanted to see, asked. “He’s not answering his phone.”

“Gee, I wonder why that would be,” he said. “Don’t you have a fiancée to plan a wedding with?”

Eddie flinched, and it made Daniel feel so very satisfied.

“Please,” the little shit begged. “I- I need to talk to him, I need to explain.”

“Good luck with that, ‘cause he’s not here.”

Eddie’s face fell at the words.

“Not here? What do you mean, not here? Where is he?”

“Pretty sure he’s somewhere in Arkansas, right now.”

“He- He left?”

Eddie looked broken about that. Broken enough that Daniel almost, almost, felt bad for him. But then he remembered how upset Evan was when he came home from their shared birthday party at the Diazes, how much he cried, and it did away with any sympathy that flared up.

“Were you expecting him to stay, Eddie?” Daniel asked. His grip on the door was so tight his hand ached. But if he let go now, he’d end up breaking his promise, along with Eddie’s face. “You’re getting married. To someone that isn't him. You expected him to stay here and watch it happen?”

“Danny, please, you don’t understand-”

“And I don’t care,” Daniel told him. Then he slammed the door closed in Eddie’s stupid face.

It was the last that he saw of Eddie Diaz for a long, long time.
