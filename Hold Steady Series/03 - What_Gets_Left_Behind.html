What Gets Left Behind

The morning after the tsunami, Eddie woke up in a bed far too small for him with his eyes puffy, his head sore, and his legs cramped. He was briefly confused, because why was he in Chris’ bed? Where was his son?

And then he remembered.

Somehow, that hurt more than when he first found his son. For the briefest of moments, he had forgotten how empty his world now was. For the briefest of moments, his son had still been alive.

He wasn’t certain how he got home, so lost was he in his grief, but he remembered that Buck had been with him the entire way. Where was Buck now? He pushed himself out of his son’s bed - his son was gone, gone, gone - and, for a moment, simply stood there. He didn’t know what to do, didn’t know how to exist after this. How had Bobby not simply ceased to be, after he lost his children?

“Buck?” he called out, his voice scratchy and his throat sore. He eased himself to his feet, his knees and back protesting his choice in sleeping arrangements. His home was quiet, far too quiet. Had his friend left? Was he still asleep?

He padded softly into the hallway, the air still.

“Buck?” he called again, to no reply. Which was concerning, because if the man was there, he would have answered by now. He checked the living room and saw the couch didn’t even have any sheets or pillows. He was about to leave, to see if Buck had taken his bed, when the smell reached his nose.

It was a coppery smell, so thick he could almost taste it.

Under the kitchen door, he could see something red pooling along the floor.

“Buck!?”

It was with reluctance and dread that he approached the door. He knew that smell, he knew what it looked like, but it couldn’t be that. It couldn’t be. He could not conceive of a reason that there would be this much blood pooling on his floor.

(Buck still hadn’t answered.)

He was careful as he eased the door open, doing his best to keep from stepping in the congealed pool of red. The moment he saw the source of it, that care went out the window. There was an arm stretched out across the floor, hand reaching out for nothing.

“Buck!” he cried, heedless of the dried blood that covered the floor as he dropped to his knees. The man was on the floor in front of the fridge, slumped onto his side. His eyes, once so very bright, were now cloudy and staring at nothing. One of the kitchen knives was laying on the floor right beside him, blood coating the blade.

“No, no, no, no. Buck, no.” He was begging, he was pleading. “Not you, too. I- No, you can’t leave me, too. No, no, no.”

Eddie’s phone was still in his pocket, and he pulled it out and started dialing in the same motion, putting it on speaker and setting it on the floor. He had rolled Buck onto his back and was just starting compressions - ”no no no not you too no” - when they answered.

“Nine-One-One, what’s your emergency?”

“M- Maddie…”

“Eddie?” The calm professionalism dropped from her tone. “Eddie, what’s wrong?”

“It- It’s Buck, he… Maddie…”

“Eddie, where are you?”

“At home, in my kitchen. Maddie, th- there’s so much blood…”

Eddie was dead. He walked, he breathed, his heart was beating, but he was dead. He was a ghost, barely floating through existence. The world turned, and he turned with it, but time meant nothing to him. The clock had broken sometime between finding Chris in that awful black tent and finding Buck in the kitchen.

At some point, paramedics had arrived to drag him away from Buck’s side. At some point, they shook their heads at each other. At some point, Eddie had bullied his way onto the ambulance with them. At some point, he was left behind in a hospital hallway as they rolled his heart away. At some point, someone in scrubs managed to move him into a chair in the waiting room.

At some point, Athena managed to find him.

She looked… Not well. Tired and broken and smaller than he had ever seen her before. The sergeant barely managed a smile at the sight of him, the motion coming out more as a grimace.

“There you are,” she said. “You weren’t home, and you left your phone behind.”

“Sorry.” He didn’t feel sorry, though. He didn’t feel much of anything. He just felt dead.

Athena let out a sigh and dropped into the seat beside him, her hand hovering in the air just above his arm, like she couldn’t decide what to do with it. Eventually, she just dropped it to pull out her phone, probably to text someone that he’d been located.

After several moments of silence, of existing in a quiet bubble in a world gone dark and cold, Eddie asked,

“Why would he do this?”

“I don’t know,” she answered.

“He left me,” he said. “Chris is- And he- He left me. I need him, and he- Why would he leave me, too?”

“I don’t know,” she repeated. And he wanted to ask if there was anything she did know, wanted to turn and scream at her for how useless she was being. But he just didn’t have the energy, couldn’t find the anger for it. He was too empty for that fire to burn.

“We need to get you changed,” Athena said.

“Why?” he asked, because he honestly couldn’t see the point.

“Honey, your pants are covered in…”

Eddie glanced down, noting with mild surprise that he was still in uniform from the day before, to see what she was talking about. From his knees down, he saw that there were shiny stains, darker than the fabric. There was some of it on his arms and hands, too, though he couldn’t remember how that had happened.

“Oh.”

“Come on, Bobby’s on his way.” She was urging him to stand. He wasn’t fighting it too much, allowing himself to be pulled up. “We’ll get you fresh clothes.”

“But, Buck…” 

“Chimney’s bringing Maddie.” Of course. They’d need her to identify the- To identify Buck. “There’s… There’s nothing here we can do.”

Eddie wasn’t certain who planned everything, because it wasn’t him. It was probably Abuela or Pepa. But a little bit over a week later, he found himself sitting in the front row as someone droned on and on about Heaven and God’s children and he just wanted them to shut up. There was no God, merciful or otherwise.  

He watched as a too small casket was lowered into the ground, and he wanted to leap in after it.

Shannon wasn’t going to have a casket, Eddie knew. She wasn’t going to be buried.

(“You can be declared legally dead seven years after going missing,” Buck had told him once, sometime after the revelation about Doug but before the bombs.)

Eddie lingered, afterwards. Truthfully, he had been lingering before, too. It’s all he did, anymore. Linger. He was a ghost, haunting wherever people left him. The men in charge of filling the hole seemed uncomfortable with his continued presence, but he couldn’t bring himself to care.

He couldn’t bring himself to care about much of anything, right now.

Like his parents.

Eddie had honestly forgotten that they were there, had honestly forgotten that they existed. But they came upon him as he watched the dirt cover his son, and they did not let him ignore them.

“This is your fault,” his father started. “You and that gringa’s. This would not have happened if you had let us keep him, like we asked. We told you, we told you, that he would have been better off with us. He would have been safer with us. Your mother and I, we told you not to drag him down, and now look what’s happened. He’s gone. Chris is dead, our grandson is dead, because you couldn’t do the right thing.”

Eddie couldn’t bring himself to argue, couldn’t find it in him to defend himself. Because there was nothing to defend. They were right, he knew.

(Buck would have been defending him, he knew. Buck would have been raging on his behalf. But Buck was gone. He had chosen to leave Eddie alone in this cold and awful world.)

At least his parents showed up for Chris’ funeral, though, unlike Buck’s. But perhaps it wasn’t a surprise, considering the Buckleys couldn’t even be bothered to show up when their son was alive.

“Their dad told her that their mother couldn’t go through this again,” Chimney told them during the visitation, in one of the rare moments that he wasn’t glued to Maddie’s side.

“Again?” Athena asked. She was holding up better than her husband, but that was a very low bar. Everyone had been taking turns essentially babysitting Bobby, doing their best to keep him from falling off the wagon. Everyone except Eddie and Chimney, that is. The latter was busy keeping Maddie afloat, and the former…

Well, a ghost was a poor babysitter.

“I don’t know, I was eavesdropping,” Chimney admitted. “Maddie was pretty upset about it, though.”

Perhaps he was still capable of feeling something, Eddie thought, because he really hated Buck’s parents.

The cemetery Maddie chose was a different one from where they buried Chris, and that rankled Eddie. The two of them should have been near each other, if not side-by-side. But they weren’t. There was an entire city between them now, there would always be a city between them, and that…

It wasn’t right.

It had been off-putting to work without Buck by his side when the man’s leg was healing, but it had been bearable. Eddie had known that it was a temporary thing, that he would get his partner back. Now, though, the station seemed emptier without the promise of Buck’s return.

They had Lena Bosko covering initially, but she moved back to the 136 when the station reopened just before Halloween. It was a rotating series of floaters after that, none of them quite working out. None of them were Buck, none of them could be Buck, and they never stuck around long enough to compete for a dead man’s place.

And they all found working with Eddie to be nearly impossible.

“You have to stop doing this,” Bobby told him, having called him into the captain’s office. It was the middle of December, Christmas fast approaching, and the absences this year were stark. They had just gotten back from a structure fire, one where Eddie had, perhaps, been a bit reckless in his decisions.

“I thought I heard something,” he said.

“You heard me telling you to get out,” the captain said. “I know you did, because Chapman heard me, and he said he was right beside you. Until you did the exact opposite of evacuating the building, and ran deeper into it, instead.”

“I’m sorry, Cap. It won’t happen again.”

“That’s what you said the last time, Eddie,” Bobby said, and exhaustion was lining the older man’s face. “And the time before that. Every single person I’ve paired you with has complained about your recklessness. About your carelessness. It’s like you’re trying to get yourself killed.”

“And you’re not?” Eddie snapped. Ever since Buck’s funeral it felt like he was always angry. Angry at himself, for not doing more to keep Chris safe. Angry at Shannon, for going to the pier with Chris that day. Angry at Buck, for leaving him alone with this.

It had been easy, so very easy, to follow Bosko’s suggestion with the fights. An excuse to make other people hurt just as much as he was hurting. An opportunity to lash out at the world and make it bleed. It had probably been inevitable, that fight where he nearly killed someone. At least he hadn’t been so lost to it all that he had let him die.

“I’m not the one being careless with my life,” Bobby retorted.

“You chose to hang around burning radioactive waste. Seemed pretty careless to me.”

The captain’s jaw moved as he ground his teeth.

“This has to stop,” he said. “Buck wouldn’t want-”

“Buck left!” Eddie shouted. “So what Buck would or wouldn’t want doesn’t really matter anymore.”

“This has to stop,” Bobby repeated. “It is going to stop. Do you understand me?”

“Crystal,” he said. “Can I go?”

He barely waited for the captain to nod his permission before storming from the office.

When Christmas came a week and a half later, it was with absolutely no cheer. They were working it that year, and Eddie was honestly extremely thankful for it. A year ago, he was taking Chris to see Santa with Buck. A year ago, he was reintroducing Chris to his mother. A year ago, his family was whole and unbroken.

A year ago, the world hadn’t been so empty.

“How’s Maddie doing?” Hen asked Chimney, while they relaxed between calls.

“About as well as you’d expect,” the other paramedic answered. “She’s missing Buck and feeling guilty for not spending Christmas with him last year.”

“I’m not sure that Buck liked Christmas,” Eddie said, trying to keep from dwelling upon what, precisely, Buck had been doing the year before instead of celebrating with any of them. “Or, maybe he used to, before that break-up.”

“The one you asked us about after the call with the crazy lady on the bridge, right?” Hen asked, and Eddie nodded.

“I wouldn’t have left him on Christmas like that,” he said, more to himself than to them. “Not on Christmas. Not ever. I wouldn’t have… I would have stayed with him. Forever.”

The paramedics glanced at each other.

“Eddie?” Hen prompted.

“I loved him.” It was the first time that Eddie had ever admitted it aloud. “I love him. I’m pretty sure Shannon knew before I did, but she never said anything.”

“He loved you, too,” she said, sounding so sure of it.

“He did,” Chimney agreed. “He told me so. Last Christmas, actually.”

“If he loved me, why did he…?” Eddie let the question trail off.

Neither of them answered.

In late January, Athena sent out an SOS to everyone. She was worried about Bobby, who had walked out and was not responding to her calls or texts. They all fanned out, searching for him in hospitals and at bars.

Eddie found him on the first try.

“Mind if I join you?” he asked, waiting until Bobby nodded before sitting on the ground beside him. The captain’s eyes were fixed on the headstone in front of him, and he was gripping tightly to a three-quarters full bottle of liquor.

“How’d you know I was here?” Bobby asked.

“It’s his birthday,” Eddie said, gesturing at the dates. “Where else would you be?”

“Makes sense,” the captain said, and took a large swallow from the bottle. “How worried is Athena?”

“Very,” he answered. “I think she has an APB out for you. I know she has the others out searching the city.”

“Are you going to tell her I’m here?”

“Not yet.”

“Are you going to stop me?” Bobby asked, and then took another drink to underline the question.

“I should,” Eddie said. “You’re going to hate yourself tomorrow.”

“I hate myself now. I keep thinking that I should have done something more.”

“Like what, Bobby? What could you have done?”

“I don’t know!” the captain snapped. “Something! Anything! There had to have been something that I missed! A sign, or- or something. He spent months spiraling at the beginning of the year, and then the truck bombing, and then- I should have realized, I should have noticed. I missed it.”

“Athena missed it, too,” Eddie pointed out. “We all missed it. And it happened in my house, Bobby. My kitchen.”

“No one blames you,” Bobby said. “You had just- No one blames you.”

“There’s no one to blame but him,” he said, and some of that constant, ever burning anger bled into his words. “It was his choice. He chose to- He chose to leave.”

“Eddie…”

“I hate him. Just a little bit. I still love him so much, but I hate him, too. Just a little.”

Eddie heard another car pull up, followed by the slamming of a car door and Athena calling Bobby’s name.

“Like I said,” he told the captain, taking the bottle from him. “It’s his birthday. Where else would you be?”

Eddie had been reckless again, though he hadn’t been careless. He had cared too much this time, perhaps. He had seen a little boy, trapped and alone and scared, and all he could think of was Chris.

It was April, nearly three whole months after Bobby fell off the wagon to land at Buck’s grave. They’d been called to help search for a missing boy, and found him stuck in an old well. Eddie had volunteered to go down after him, had insisted upon it, really. Everything had been fine, at the start. They lowered him down, he spotted Hayden and had been just about able to reach him.

And then they started pulling him back up.

And then he cut his line.

Maybe- No, it definitely wasn’t the smartest of decisions. It was the same kind of reckless stunt that Buck would have done, and then be chewed out by Bobby for. But Hayden had been just within reach, and all Eddie could think of was that horrible, Chris-shaped emptiness he still carried around, and how he did not wish that upon anyone else.

They sent Chimney down after him. The man had been relieved that nothing had actually happened to him. They got Hayden prepped to be carried back up, the paramedic quipped about not cutting the line they were going to send down to him.

And then, shortly after Chimney took Hayden up, something happened. Eddie wasn’t entirely certain what, but the hole they had dug to reach Hayden collapsed in on itself, nearly burying him alive. He got no replies on his radio when he tried to reach them.

“I’m still alive down here!” he shouted into it. “Can anybody hear me!? I’m still alive down here!”

Silence, silence, nothing but silence.

Eddie was alone, and no one was coming, and the water was rising. He knew, he knew, he needed to find a way out. Before the water reached over his head, before the oxygen in his little hole ran out.

But why should he?

At the bottom of a collapsed well or in his empty house, Eddie was going to be alone, regardless. Chris was gone, and Shannon was gone, and Buck was gone. There was no one waiting for him, no one who needed him.

Perhaps, he thought, if there was a God and there was a Heaven, he could see them again.

Perhaps, he thought, it would have been better if he had bled out in the desert.

Perhaps, he thought, if he just lay there and waited, then-

Ichabod had let this particular branch play out for far longer than he had the others that had sprouted from Buck’s many deaths. Dead things, as he had told him, might not have been entertaining, but what was left behind very much was. And this time it had been very, very entertaining. Up until it got boring, at least. 

Which meant it was time to trim it away.

He did always try to clean up after himself, and to keep things from becoming too cluttered.
